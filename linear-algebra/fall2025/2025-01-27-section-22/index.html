Definition 2.2.1
Orthogonal Projections
Consider a line $L$ in the coordinate plane, running through the origin. Any vector $\vec{x}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ can be written uniquely as $\vec{x}=\vec{x}^{\parallel}+\vec{x}^{\perp}$, where $\vec{x}^{\parallel}$ is parallel to $L$ and $\vec{x}^{\perp}$ is perpendicular to $L$.
The transformation $T(\vec{x})=\vec{x}^{\parallel}$ is called the orthogonal projection of $\vec{x}$ onto $L$, denoted $\text{proj}_L(\vec{x})$. If $\vec{w}$ is a nonzero vector parallel to $L$, then $$\text{proj}_L(\vec{x})=\left(\frac{\vec{x}\cdot\vec{w}}{\vec{w}\cdot\vec{w}}\right)\vec{w}.$$ In particular, if $\vec{u}=\begin{pmatrix}u_1\\u_2\end{pmatrix}$ is a unit vector parallel to $L$, this is a linear transformation with matrix $$P=\begin{pmatrix} u_1^2 & u_1u_2 \\ u_1u_2 & u_2^2 \end{pmatrix}.$$ Drag $\vec{w}$ to change the line $L$ and drag $\vec{x}$ to see $\text{proj}_L(\vec{x})$ (orange) update live — the red point previews the reflection $\text{ref}_L(\vec{x})$ from the next definition. Example 2
Find the matrix of the projection onto a line
Find the matrix $P$ of the orthogonal projection onto the line $L$ spanned by $\vec{w}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\end{pmatrix}$.

Solution. $$P=\frac{1}{w_1^2+w_2^2}\begin{pmatrix} w_1^2 & w_1w_2 \\ w_1w_2 & w_2^2 \end{pmatrix} =\frac{1}{25}\begin{pmatrix} 9 & 12 \\ 12 & 16 \end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix} 0.36 & 0.48 \\ 0.48 & 0.64 \end{pmatrix}.$$ Drag $\vec{w}$ in the applet above to $(3,4)$ to check this against the picture.
Definition 2.2.2
Reflections
Consider a line $L$ through the origin and $\vec{x}=\vec{x}^{\parallel}+\vec{x}^{\perp}$ as before. The reflection of $\vec{x}$ about $L$, denoted $\text{ref}_L(\vec{x})$, is $$\text{ref}_L(\vec{x})=\vec{x}^{\parallel}-\vec{x}^{\perp}.$$ Adding $\vec{x}=\vec{x}^{\parallel}+\vec{x}^{\perp}$ and this equation gives $\vec{x}+\text{ref}_L(\vec{x}) =2\vec{x}^{\parallel}=2\text{proj}_L(\vec{x})$, so $$\text{ref}_L(\vec{x})=2\text{proj}_L(\vec{x})-\vec{x}=(2P-I_2)\vec{x}.$$ The matrix $S=2P-I_2$ is always of the form $$S=\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & -a \end{pmatrix},\qquad a^2+b^2=1,$$ and, conversely, any matrix of this form represents a reflection about a line. The red point in the applet above is $\text{ref}_L(\vec{x})$.

Example 3
The same ideas extend to $\mathbb{R}^3$. If $L^\perp=V$ is the plane through the origin perpendicular to a line $L$ with unit direction $\vec{u}$, then $$\text{proj}_V(\vec{x})=\vec{x}-(\vec{x}\cdot\vec{u})\vec{u}, \qquad \text{ref}_V(\vec{x})=\vec{x}-2(\vec{x}\cdot\vec{u})\vec{u}.$$
Example. Let $V$ be the plane $2x_1+x_2-2x_3=0$, and let $\vec{x}=\begin{pmatrix}5\\4\\-2 \end{pmatrix}$. Find $\text{ref}_V(\vec{x})$.

Solution. The vector $\vec{v}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix}$ is perpendicular to $V$ (its components are the coefficients of the plane's equation), so $\vec{u}=\frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix}$ is a unit normal. Then $$\text{ref}_V(\vec{x})=\begin{pmatrix}5\\4\\-2\end{pmatrix} -\frac{2}{9}\left(\begin{pmatrix}5\\4\\-2\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix}\right) \begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}5\\4\\-2\end{pmatrix}-4\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}-3\\0\\6\end{pmatrix}.$$
Theorem 2.2.3
Rotations
The matrix of a counterclockwise rotation in $\mathbb{R}^2$ through an angle $\theta$ is $$\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}.$$ This matrix has the form $\begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}$ with $a^2+b^2=1$; conversely, any matrix of that form represents a rotation.

Example 4. The matrix of a counterclockwise rotation through $\pi/6$ ($30^\circ$) is $$\begin{pmatrix} \cos(\pi/6) & -\sin(\pi/6) \\ \sin(\pi/6) & \cos(\pi/6) \end{pmatrix} =\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{3} & -1 \\ 1 & \sqrt{3} \end{pmatrix}.$$ Set $a=\cos\theta,\,b=-\sin\theta,\,c=\sin\theta,\,d=\cos\theta$ in the Example 1 applet to watch any rotation in action.
Theorem 2.2.4
Rotations combined with a scaling
A matrix of the form $\begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}$ represents a rotation combined with a scaling. More precisely, if $r$ and $\theta$ are the polar coordinates of the vector $\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}$, then $$\begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix}=r\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$$ represents a rotation through $\theta$ combined with a scaling by $r$. This is part (f) of Example 1: the matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1 & -1\\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ has $a=b=1$, so $r=\sqrt{2}$ and $\theta=\pi/4$.
Theorem 2.2.5
Horizontal and vertical shears
The matrix of a horizontal shear is of the form $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and the matrix of a vertical shear is of the form $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ k & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, where $k$ is an arbitrary constant.
Picture a deck of cards on a ruler: tilting the ruler while the cards stay vertical slides each card up by an amount proportional to its horizontal distance from the pivot — that's a vertical shear. Try $a=1,\,b=0,\,c=k,\,d=1$ in the Example 1 applet for a few values of $k$.
Summary
Matrices of the geometric linear transformations of $\mathbb{R}^2$
Transformation Matrix
Scaling by $k$ $kI_2=\begin{pmatrix}k&0\\0&k\end{pmatrix}$
Orthogonal projection onto line $L$ $\begin{pmatrix}u_1^2&u_1u_2\\u_1u_2&u_2^2\end{pmatrix}$, $\vec{u}=\begin{pmatrix}u_1\\u_2 \end{pmatrix}$ a unit vector parallel to $L$

Vertical shear $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\k&1\end{pmatrix}$
