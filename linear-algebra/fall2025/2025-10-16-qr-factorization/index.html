The Gram-Schmidt Process
Low-dimensional cases (Figures 1 to 3)

In Theorem 5.1.5, we gave a formula for the orthogonal projection onto a subspace \(V\) of \(\mathbb{R}^n\),

$$\text{proj}_V\vec x=(\vec u_1\cdot\vec x)\vec u_1+\cdots+(\vec u_m\cdot\vec x)\vec u_m,$$

where \(\vec u_1,\dots,\vec u_m\) is an orthonormal basis of \(V\). We now construct such a basis: an algorithm that converts any basis \(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_m\) of \(V\) into an orthonormal basis \(\vec u_1,\dots,\vec u_m\) of \(V\).

\(V\) is a line with basis \(\vec v_1\)\(\vec u_1=\dfrac{1}{\|\vec v_1\|}\vec v_1\)
\(V\) is a plane with basis \(\vec v_1,\vec v_2\). Resolve \(\vec v_2\) relative to the line \(L\) spanned by \(\vec u_1\)\(\vec v_2=\vec v_2^{\parallel}+\vec v_2^{\perp}\)
\(\vec v_2^{\perp}=\)\(\vec v_2-\vec v_2^{\parallel}=\vec v_2-\text{proj}_L(\vec v_2)=\vec v_2-(\vec u_1\cdot\vec v_2)\vec u_1\)
\(\vec u_2=\)\(\dfrac{1}{\|\vec v_2^{\perp}\|}\vec v_2^{\perp}\)
Example 1
An orthonormal basis of a plane in \(\mathbb{R}^4\), and the change of basis matrix

a. Find an orthonormal basis \(\vec u_1,\vec u_2\) of the subspace \(V=\text{span}\left(\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1\\9\\9\\1\end{bmatrix}\right)\) of \(\mathbb{R}^4\), with basis \(\vec v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}\), \(\vec v_2=\begin{bmatrix}1\\9\\9\\1\end{bmatrix}\).

b. Find the change of basis matrix \(R\) from the basis \(\mathfrak{B}=(\vec v_1,\vec v_2)\) to the basis \(\mathfrak{A}=(\vec u_1,\vec u_2)\) you constructed in part a.

a. \(\vec u_1=\dfrac{1}{\|\vec v_1\|}\vec v_1\)\(\dfrac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1/2\\1/2\\1/2\\1/2\end{bmatrix}\)
\(\vec v_2^{\perp}=\vec v_2-\vec v_2^{\parallel}=\vec v_2-(\vec u_1\cdot\vec v_2)\vec u_1\)\(\begin{bmatrix}1\\9\\9\\1\end{bmatrix}-10\begin{bmatrix}1/2\\1/2\\1/2\\1/2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-4\\4\\4\\-4\end{bmatrix}\)
\(\vec u_2=\dfrac{1}{\|\vec v_2^{\perp}\|}\vec v_2^{\perp}\)\(\dfrac{1}{8}\begin{bmatrix}-4\\4\\4\\-4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1/2\\1/2\\1/2\\-1/2\end{bmatrix}\)
b. The columns of \(R\) are the coordinate vectors of \(\vec v_1\) and \(\vec v_2\) with respect to \(\mathfrak{A}\)\(\vec v_1=2\vec u_1\) and \(\vec v_2=10\vec u_1+8\vec u_2\), so \(R=\begin{bmatrix}2&10\\0&8\end{bmatrix}\)
\(\begin{bmatrix}\vec v_1&\vec v_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec u_1&\vec u_2\end{bmatrix}R\)\(\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&9\\1&9\\1&1\end{bmatrix}}_{M}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1/2&-1/2\\1/2&1/2\\1/2&1/2\\1/2&-1/2\end{bmatrix}}_{Q}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}2&10\\0&8\end{bmatrix}}_{R}\)

In this context, it is customary to denote the matrices on the right-hand side by \(Q\) and \(R\). \(M\) (4 by 2) is written as the product of \(Q\) (4 by 2) with orthonormal columns and the upper triangular \(R\) (2 by 2) with positive entries on the diagonal. This is referred to as the \(QR\) factorization of \(M\).

Three Dimensions
Resolve \(\vec v_3\) relative to a plane (Figure 4)

How do we proceed for a three-dimensional subspace \(V\) of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) with basis \(\vec v_1,\vec v_2,\vec v_3\)? First find an orthonormal basis \(\vec u_1,\vec u_2\) of the plane \(E=\text{span}(\vec v_1,\vec v_2)\), as in Example 1. Next resolve \(\vec v_3\) into its components parallel and perpendicular to the plane \(E\).

\(\vec v_3=\)\(\vec v_3^{\parallel}+\vec v_3^{\perp}\)
\(\vec v_3^{\perp}=\vec v_3-\vec v_3^{\parallel}=\vec v_3-\text{proj}_E(\vec v_3)=\)\(\vec v_3-(\vec u_1\cdot\vec v_3)\vec u_1-(\vec u_2\cdot\vec v_3)\vec u_2\)
\(\vec u_3=\)\(\dfrac{1}{\|\vec v_3^{\perp}\|}\vec v_3^{\perp}\)
Theorem 5.2.1
The Gram-Schmidt process
Theorem 5.2.1: The Gram-Schmidt process

Consider a basis \(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_m\) of a subspace \(V\) of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). For \(j=2,\dots,m\), we resolve the vector \(\vec v_j\) into its components parallel and perpendicular to the span of the preceding vectors, \(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_{j-1}\):

$$\vec v_j=\vec v_j^{\parallel}+\vec v_j^{\perp},\qquad\text{with respect to }\text{span}(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_{j-1}).$$

Then

$$\vec u_1=\frac{1}{\|\vec v_1\|}\vec v_1,\quad \vec u_2=\frac{1}{\|\vec v_2^{\perp}\|}\vec v_2^{\perp},\ \dots,\ \vec u_j=\frac{1}{\|\vec v_j^{\perp}\|}\vec v_j^{\perp},\ \dots,\ \vec u_m=\frac{1}{\|\vec v_m^{\perp}\|}\vec v_m^{\perp}$$

is an orthonormal basis of \(V\). By Theorem 5.1.7, we have

$$\vec v_j^{\perp}=\vec v_j-\vec v_j^{\parallel}=\vec v_j-(\vec u_1\cdot\vec v_j)\vec u_1-\cdots-(\vec u_{j-1}\cdot\vec v_j)\vec u_{j-1}.$$

Keep computing perpendicular components using the projection formula, and keep dividing vectors by their length.

The QR Factorization
Change of basis from \(\mathfrak{B}\) to \(\mathfrak{A}\)

The Gram-Schmidt process represents a change of basis from the "old" basis \(\mathfrak{B}=(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_m)\) to a "new" orthonormal basis \(\mathfrak{A}=(\vec u_1,\dots,\vec u_m)\) of \(V\). It is most succinctly described by the change of basis matrix \(R\) from \(\mathfrak{B}\) to \(\mathfrak{A}\) (Theorem 4.3.4):

$$\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\vert&&\vert\\ \vec v_1&\cdots&\vec v_m\\ \vert&&\vert\end{bmatrix}}_{M}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\vert&&\vert\\ \vec u_1&\cdots&\vec u_m\\ \vert&&\vert\end{bmatrix}}_{Q}R$$

The preceding equation is called the \(QR\) factorization of \(M\). The relationship among \(M\), \(Q\) and \(R\), where \(\vec x\) is a vector in \(V\):

\([\vec x]_{\mathfrak{A}}\)
\(Q\)⟶
\(\vec x\)
\(R\)↑
∥
\([\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}}\)
\(M\)⟶
\(\vec x\)

Theorem 5.2.2
\(QR\) factorization

The \(j\)th column of \(R\) holds the coordinates of \(\vec v_j\) with respect to \(\mathfrak{A}\):

$$\vec v_j=\vec v_j^{\parallel}+\vec v_j^{\perp}=r_{1j}\vec u_1+\cdots+r_{ij}\vec u_i+\cdots+r_{j-1,j}\vec u_{j-1}+\|\vec v_j^{\perp}\|\,\vec u_j.$$
\(r_{ij}=\) if \(i<j\)\(\vec u_i\cdot\vec v_j\)
\(r_{jj}=\)\(\|\vec v_j^{\perp}\|\)
\(r_{ij}=\) if \(i>j\)\(0\): \(R\) is upper triangular, and \(r_{11}=\|\vec v_1\|\)
Theorem 5.2.2: QR factorization

Consider an \(n\times m\) matrix \(M\) with linearly independent columns \(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_m\). Then there exists an \(n\times m\) matrix \(Q\) whose columns \(\vec u_1,\dots,\vec u_m\) are orthonormal and an upper triangular \(m\times m\) matrix \(R\) with positive diagonal entries such that

$$M=QR.$$

This representation is unique. Furthermore, \(r_{11}=\|\vec v_1\|\), \(r_{jj}=\|\vec v_j^{\perp}\|\) (for \(j=2,\dots,m\)), and \(r_{ij}=\vec u_i\cdot\vec v_j\) (for \(i<j\)).

Example 2
Find the \(QR\) factorization of \(M\)

Find the \(QR\) factorization of the matrix \(M=\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\1&7\\-2&-8\end{bmatrix}\), with \(\vec v_1=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\-2\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\vec v_2=\begin{bmatrix}2\\7\\-8\end{bmatrix}\).

The factorization has the form \(M=\begin{bmatrix}\vec v_1&\vec v_2\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\vec u_1&\vec u_2\end{bmatrix}}_{Q}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\|\vec v_1\|&\vec u_1\cdot\vec v_2\\0&\|\vec v_2^{\perp}\|\end{bmatrix}}_{R}\).

\(r_{11}=\|\vec v_1\|\), \(\vec u_1=\dfrac{1}{r_{11}}\vec v_1\)\(r_{11}=3\), \(\vec u_1=\dfrac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\-2\end{bmatrix}\)
\(r_{12}=\vec u_1\cdot\vec v_2\), \(\vec v_2^{\perp}=\vec v_2-r_{12}\vec u_1\)\(r_{12}=9\), \(\vec v_2^{\perp}=\begin{bmatrix}-4\\4\\-2\end{bmatrix}\)
\(r_{22}=\|\vec v_2^{\perp}\|\), \(\vec u_2=\dfrac{1}{r_{22}}\vec v_2^{\perp}\)\(r_{22}=\sqrt{36}=6\), \(\vec u_2=\dfrac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}-2\\2\\-1\end{bmatrix}\)
\(M=QR\)\(\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\1&7\\-2&-8\end{bmatrix}=\left(\dfrac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}2&-2\\1&2\\-2&-1\end{bmatrix}\right)\begin{bmatrix}3&9\\0&6\end{bmatrix}\)
Theorem 5.2.3
\(QR\) factorization: the algorithm
Theorem 5.2.3: QR factorization

Consider an \(n\times m\) matrix \(M\) with linearly independent columns \(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_m\). Then the columns \(\vec u_1,\dots,\vec u_m\) of \(Q\) and the entries \(r_{ij}\) of \(R\) can be computed in the following order:

first column of \(R\), first column of \(Q\); second column of \(R\), second column of \(Q\); third column of \(R\), third column of \(Q\); and so on. More specifically,

$$r_{11}=\|\vec v_1\|,\qquad \vec u_1=\frac{1}{r_{11}}\vec v_1;$$ $$r_{12}=\vec u_1\cdot\vec v_2,\quad \vec v_2^{\perp}=\vec v_2-r_{12}\vec u_1,\quad r_{22}=\|\vec v_2^{\perp}\|,\quad \vec u_2=\frac{1}{r_{22}}\vec v_2^{\perp};$$ $$r_{13}=\vec u_1\cdot\vec v_3,\quad r_{23}=\vec u_2\cdot\vec v_3,\quad \vec v_3^{\perp}=\vec v_3-r_{13}\vec u_1-r_{23}\vec u_2,\quad r_{33}=\|\vec v_3^{\perp}\|,\quad \vec u_3=\frac{1}{r_{33}}\vec v_3^{\perp};$$

and so on.

Example 3
A \(3\times3\) matrix

Find the \(QR\) factorization of \(M=\begin{bmatrix}2&2&0\\1&7&0\\-2&-8&3\end{bmatrix}\). Its first two columns are those of Example 2.

First column of \(R\) and \(Q\)\(r_{11}=3\), \(\vec u_1=\dfrac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\-2\end{bmatrix}\)
Second column\(r_{12}=9\), \(r_{22}=6\), \(\vec u_2=\dfrac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}-2\\2\\-1\end{bmatrix}\)
\(r_{13}=\vec u_1\cdot\vec v_3\), \(r_{23}=\vec u_2\cdot\vec v_3\)\(r_{13}=-2\), \(r_{23}=-1\)
\(\vec v_3^{\perp}=\vec v_3-r_{13}\vec u_1-r_{23}\vec u_2\)\(\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\3\end{bmatrix}+\dfrac{2}{3}\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\-2\end{bmatrix}+\dfrac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}-2\\2\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\dfrac{2}{3}\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{bmatrix}\)

\(M=QR\)\(\begin{bmatrix}2&2&0\\1&7&0\\-2&-8&3\end{bmatrix}=\left(\dfrac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix}2&-2&1\\1&2&2\\-2&-1&2\end{bmatrix}\right)\begin{bmatrix}3&9&-2\\0&6&-1\\0&0&2\end{bmatrix}\)
