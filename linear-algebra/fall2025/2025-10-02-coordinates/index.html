Coordinates

Definition 3.4.1
Coordinates in a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^n\)

Let \(\mathfrak{B}=(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_m)\) be a basis of a subspace \(V\) of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Every \(\vec x\) in \(V\) can be written uniquely as

$$\vec x=c_1\vec v_1+c_2\vec v_2+\cdots+c_m\vec v_m .$$

The \(c_i\) are the \(\mathfrak{B}\)-coordinates of \(\vec x\), and $$[\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\ \vdots\\ c_m\end{bmatrix}$$ is the \(\mathfrak{B}\)-coordinate vector: the "address" of \(\vec x\) in the grid made by the basis.

$$\vec x=S\,[\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}},\qquad S=\begin{bmatrix}|&&|\\ \vec v_1&\cdots&\vec v_m\\ |&&|\end{bmatrix}\ (n\times m).$$

Example 1. \(\vec v_1=(1,1,1)\), \(\vec v_2=(1,2,3)\), \(V=\operatorname{span}(\vec v_1,\vec v_2)\). Is \(\vec x=(5,7,9)\) in \(V\)?

Solve \(c_1\vec v_1+c_2\vec v_2=\vec x\)\(\operatorname{rref}\begin{bmatrix}1&1&5\\1&2&7\\1&3&9\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&3\\0&1&2\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\): consistent, \(c_1=3,\ c_2=2\)
Is \(\vec x\) in \(V\)? Its coordinates?Yes: \(\vec x=3\vec v_1+2\vec v_2\), so \([\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix}\)
Check \(\vec x=S[\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}}\)\(\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&2\\1&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5\\7\\9\end{bmatrix}\)
Example 2
Finding coordinates, and building a vector from its coordinates

Basis \(\vec v_1=(3,1)\) (blue), \(\vec v_2=(-1,3)\) (green). The tilted grid is the \(c_1\)-\(c_2\) coordinate system. Drag \(\vec x\) and read its address \([\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}}\) from the grid.

(a) \(\vec x=(10,10)\). Find \([\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}}\).Solve \(c_1(3,1)+c_2(-1,3)=(10,10)\): \(c_1=4,\ c_2=2\), so \([\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}4\\2\end{bmatrix}\). Or \(S^{-1}\vec x=\tfrac1{10}\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\-1&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}10\\10\end{bmatrix}\).
(b) \([\vec y]_{\mathfrak{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\-1\end{bmatrix}\). Find \(\vec y\).\(\vec y=2\vec v_1-\vec v_2=2\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}-1\\3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}7\\-1\end{bmatrix}\). Or \(\vec y=S[\vec y]_{\mathfrak{B}}\).

The axes need not be perpendicular: Cartesian coordinates work just as well with oblique axes.

Theorem 3.4.2
Linearity of coordinates

If \(\mathfrak{B}\) is a basis of a subspace \(V\) of \(\mathbb{R}^n\), then for all \(\vec x,\vec y\) in \(V\) and all scalars \(k\):

$$[\vec x+\vec y]_{\mathfrak{B}}=[\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}}+[\vec y]_{\mathfrak{B}},\qquad [k\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}}=k\,[\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}}.$$

Test it with Example 2: \([\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}}=(4,2)\) for \(\vec x=(10,10)\) and \([\vec y]_{\mathfrak{B}}=(2,-1)\) for \(\vec y=(7,-1)\).

\(\vec x+\vec y\)\((17,9)\), and \(6\vec v_1+1\vec v_2=(18-1,\,6+3)=(17,9)\). So \([\vec x+\vec y]_{\mathfrak{B}}=(6,1)=(4,2)+(2,-1)\).
\(3\vec x\)\([3\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}}=3(4,2)=(12,6)\)
Why does this work?Coordinates are just the coefficients of the unique linear combination. Adding vectors adds coefficients, scaling a vector scales them.
Theorem 3.4.3
The \(\mathfrak{B}\)-matrix of a linear transformation

Let \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n\) be linear and \(\mathfrak{B}=(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_n)\) a basis. There is a unique \(n\times n\) matrix \(B\) with

$$[T(\vec x)]_{\mathfrak{B}}=B\,[\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}},\qquad B=\begin{bmatrix}|&&|\\ [T(\vec v_1)]_{\mathfrak{B}}&\cdots&[T(\vec v_n)]_{\mathfrak{B}}\\ |&&|\end{bmatrix}.$$

Example 3. \(T\) = orthogonal projection onto the line \(L\) spanned by \(\vec v_1=(3,1)\), with \(\vec v_2=(-1,3)\perp L\). Drag \(\vec x\): the purple \(T(\vec x)\) always lands on the \(c_1\)-axis.

\(T(\vec v_1)\) and \([T(\vec v_1)]_{\mathfrak{B}}\)\(\vec v_1\) is on \(L\), so \(T(\vec v_1)=\vec v_1\) and \([T(\vec v_1)]_{\mathfrak{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}\)
\(T(\vec v_2)\) and \([T(\vec v_2)]_{\mathfrak{B}}\)\(\vec v_2\perp L\), so \(T(\vec v_2)=\vec 0\) and \([T(\vec v_2)]_{\mathfrak{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}\)
The matrix \(B\)\(B=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\): "keep \(c_1\), kill \(c_2\)". Much simpler than the standard matrix.
Theorem 3.4.4
Standard matrix versus \(\mathfrak{B}\)-matrix

Let \(A\) be the standard matrix of \(T\) and \(B\) its \(\mathfrak{B}\)-matrix. With \(S=[\vec v_1\ \cdots\ \vec v_n]\),

$$AS=SB,\qquad B=S^{-1}AS,\qquad A=SBS^{-1}.$$

The diagram says it all: going around either way from \([\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}}\) to \(T(\vec x)\) must give the same result.

$$\begin{array}{ccc}\vec x&\xrightarrow{\ \ A\ \ }&T(\vec x)\\ {\scriptstyle S}\ \uparrow&&\uparrow\ {\scriptstyle S}\\ [\vec x]_{\mathfrak{B}}&\xrightarrow{\ \ B\ \ }&[T(\vec x)]_{\mathfrak{B}}\end{array}$$

Example 5 (continued). Projection onto \(L\), \(\mathfrak{B}=((3,1),(-1,3))\), \(B=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)\). Recover \(A\).

\(S\) and \(S^{-1}\)\(S=\begin{bmatrix}3&-1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}\), \(S^{-1}=\dfrac1{10}\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\-1&3\end{bmatrix}\)
\(A=SBS^{-1}\)\(\begin{bmatrix}3&-1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\dfrac1{10}\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\-1&3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.9&0.3\\0.3&0.1\end{bmatrix}\), the matrix in the picture
Definition 3.4.5 and Theorem 3.4.6
Similar matrices

\(A\) is similar to \(B\) if there is an invertible \(S\) with \(AS=SB\), i.e. \(B=S^{-1}AS\). In words: two matrices are similar when they represent the same transformation in different bases.

Example 6. Is \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\4&3\end{bmatrix}\) similar to \(B=\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}\)? Start with the standard basis and drag the tips of \(\vec v_1\) to \((1,2)\) and \(\vec v_2\) to \((1,-1)\).

Which \(S\) works?\(S=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\2&-1\end{bmatrix}\), \(\det S=-3\neq0\). Check \(AS=\begin{bmatrix}5&-1\\10&1\end{bmatrix}=SB\).
Theorem 3.4.6: similarity is an equivalence relation. Why reflexive, symmetric, transitive?\(A=I^{-1}AI\). If \(B=S^{-1}AS\) then \(A=SBS^{-1}\). If \(B=P^{-1}AP\) and \(C=Q^{-1}BQ\) then \(C=(PQ)^{-1}A(PQ)\).
Theorem 3.4.7
When is the \(\mathfrak{B}\)-matrix of \(T\) diagonal?

For \(T(\vec x)=A\vec x\) and a basis \(\mathfrak{B}=(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_n)\) of \(\mathbb{R}^n\), the \(\mathfrak{B}\)-matrix \(B\) is diagonal if and only if

$$T(\vec v_1)=c_1\vec v_1,\ \ \dots,\ \ T(\vec v_n)=c_n\vec v_n .$$

Geometrically: each \(T(\vec v_j)\) is parallel to \(\vec v_j\).

Example 9. \(T\) = reflection about the line \(L\) spanned by \((2,1)\). Drag \(\vec v_1\) to \((2,1)\) and \(\vec v_2\) to \((-1,2)\), and watch \(B\).

Which vectors does a reflection keep on their own line?Vectors along \(L\) (\(T(\vec v_1)=\vec v_1\)) and vectors perpendicular to \(L\) (\(T(\vec v_2)=-\vec v_2\)).
So the \(\mathfrak{B}\)-matrix is\(B=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}\)
Example 10
A transformation that has no such basis

\(A=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\) is the rotation through \(\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\). Is there a basis \(\mathfrak{B}\) with a diagonal \(B\)?

Try to make \(B\) diagonal by dragging \(\vec v_1\) and \(\vec v_2\).

What would we need?Nonzero \(\vec v_j\) with \(T(\vec v_j)\) parallel to \(\vec v_j\) (Theorem 3.4.7).
Why is it impossible?A quarter turn sends every nonzero \(\vec x\) to a vector perpendicular to it, never parallel. No such basis exists.
