Finding the Eigenvectors of a Matrix

MATH 3330 · Dr. Esteban Diaz
Eigenspaces
Definition 7.3.1 and Example 1

Having found an eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\), we now turn to the corresponding eigenvectors: the vectors \(\vec v\) in \(\mathbb R^n\) such that \(A\vec v=\lambda\vec v\), or \((A-\lambda I_n)\vec v=\vec 0\).

In other words, we have to find thekernel of the matrix \(A-\lambda I_n\)
Definition 7.3.1: Eigenspaces

Consider an eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\). Then the kernel of the matrix \(A-\lambda I_n\) is called the eigenspace associated with \(\lambda\), denoted by \(E_\lambda\):

$$E_\lambda=\ker(A-\lambda I_n)=\{\vec v\text{ in }\mathbb R^n:\ A\vec v=\lambda\vec v\}.$$
The eigenvectors with eigenvalue \(\lambda\) arethe nonzero vectors in the eigenspace \(E_\lambda\)

Example 1. Let \(T(\vec x)=A\vec x\) be the orthogonal projection onto a plane \(V\) in \(\mathbb R^3\). Describe the eigenspaces \(E_1\) and \(E_0\) geometrically. Is matrix \(A\) diagonalizable?

\(E_1\) consists of the solutions of \(A\vec v=1\vec v=\vec v\). These arethe vectors on the plane \(V\). So \(E_1=V\).
\(E_0=\ker A\) consists of the solutions of \(A\vec v=0\vec v=\vec 0\). These arethe vectors on the line \(V^\perp\) perpendicular to the plane \(V\). So \(E_0=V^\perp\).
An eigenbasis \(\vec v_1,\vec v_2,\vec v_3\) for \(A\)Choose two linearly independent vectors \(\vec v_1,\vec v_2\) in \(E_1=V\) and a nonzero vector \(\vec v_3\) in \(E_0=V^\perp\)
Is \(A\) diagonalizable? To what matrix \(B\) is it similar?Yes: \(A\) is similar to \(B=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\)

Example 2

To find the eigenvectors associated with a known eigenvalue \(\lambda\) algebraically, we seek a basis of the eigenspace \(E_\lambda=\ker(A-\lambda I_n)\), a problem we can handle (Section 3.3).

Example 2. Find the eigenspaces of the matrix \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\4&3\end{bmatrix}\). Diagonalize matrix \(A\) if you can.

In Example 1 of Section 7.2 we saw that the eigenvalues are\(5\) and \(-1\)
\(E_5=\ker(A-5I_2)=\ker\begin{bmatrix}-4&2\\4&-2\end{bmatrix}\). Finding the kernel amounts to finding the relations between the columns; for a \(2\times2\) matrix this can be done by inspection (the Kyle numbers). \(E_5=\)\(\operatorname{span}\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}\), since \(1\cdot\begin{bmatrix}-4\\4\end{bmatrix}+2\cdot\begin{bmatrix}2\\-2\end{bmatrix}=\vec 0\)
\(E_{-1}=\ker(A+I_2)=\ker\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\4&4\end{bmatrix}=\)\(\operatorname{span}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}\)
Check that these are eigenvectors: \(A\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}\) and \(A\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}\)\(\begin{bmatrix}5\\10\end{bmatrix}=5\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}\) and \(\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}=(-1)\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}\)
Both eigenspaces are lines. Is there an eigenbasis? Find \(S\) and \(B\).Yes: \(\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}\). So \(A\) is diagonalizable with \(S=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\2&-1\end{bmatrix}\) and \(B=\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}\).
For peace of mind, check \(AS=SB\)\(AS=\begin{bmatrix}5&-1\\10&1\end{bmatrix}=SB\)
Geometrically, \(A\) representsa scaling by a factor of \(5\) along the line spanned by \(\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}\), while the line spanned by \(\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}\) is flipped over the origin
A Matrix That Fails to Be Diagonalizable
Example 3

Example 3. Find the eigenspaces of

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\0&0&1\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

Diagonalize matrix \(A\) if you can.

The eigenvalues of the upper triangular matrix \(A\) are the diagonal entries\(1\) and \(0\), with algebraic multiplicities \(2\) and \(1\), respectively
\(E_1=\ker(A-I_3)=\ker\begin{bmatrix}0&1&1\\0&-1&1\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}=\)\(\operatorname{span}\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{bmatrix}\)
\(E_0=\ker A=\ker\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1\\0&0&1\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}=\)\(\operatorname{span}\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}\)
Both eigenspaces are lines in the \(x_1\)-\(x_2\)-plane. How many linearly independent eigenvectors can we find?Only two, one in each of \(E_0\) and \(E_1\). We are unable to construct an eigenbasis for \(A\). Thus, \(A\) fails to be diagonalizable.
Compare with Example 1 (the projection onto a plane): there too we had eigenvalues \(1\) and \(0\). What is different?In Example 1 the eigenspace \(E_1\) was a plane, while both eigenspaces here turn out to be lines
Geometric Multiplicity
Definition 7.3.2
Definition 7.3.2: Geometric multiplicity

Consider an eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\). The dimension of the eigenspace \(E_\lambda=\ker(A-\lambda I_n)\) is called the geometric multiplicity of eigenvalue \(\lambda\), denoted by \(\operatorname{gemu}(\lambda)\). Thus,

$$\operatorname{gemu}(\lambda)=\operatorname{nullity}(A-\lambda I_n)=n-\operatorname{rank}(A-\lambda I_n).$$
Example 3: \(\operatorname{almu}(1)=\)\(2\)
but \(\operatorname{gemu}(1)=\dim E_1=\)\(1\). The geometric multiplicity of an eigenvalue may be different from its algebraic multiplicity.
Example 1 (projection): \(\operatorname{gemu}(1)\) and \(\operatorname{gemu}(0)\)\(2\) and \(1\), adding up to \(n=3\)
Example 2: \(\operatorname{gemu}(5)\) and \(\operatorname{gemu}(-1)\)\(1\) and \(1\), adding up to \(n=2\)
Example 3: \(\operatorname{gemu}(1)\) and \(\operatorname{gemu}(0)\)\(1\) and \(1\), adding up to \(2<n=3\)

Eigenbases and Geometric Multiplicities
Theorems 7.3.3 and 7.3.4, Example 4
Theorem 7.3.3: Eigenbases and geometric multiplicities

a. Consider an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\). If we find a basis of each eigenspace of \(A\) and concatenate all these bases, then the resulting eigenvectors \(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_s\) will be linearly independent. (Note that \(s\) is the sum of the geometric multiplicities of \(A\).) This result implies that \(s\le n\).

b. Matrix \(A\) is diagonalizable if (and only if) the geometric multiplicities of the eigenvalues add up to \(n\) (meaning that \(s=n\) in part a).

Theorem 7.3.4: An \(n\times n\) matrix with \(n\) distinct eigenvalues

If an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\) has \(n\) distinct eigenvalues, then \(A\) is diagonalizable. We can construct an eigenbasis by finding an eigenvector for each eigenvalue.

Example 4. Is the following matrix \(A\) diagonalizable?

$$A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3&4&5&6\\0&2&3&4&5&6\\0&0&3&4&5&6\\0&0&0&4&5&6\\0&0&0&0&5&6\\0&0&0&0&0&6\end{bmatrix}$$
Diagonalizable?Yes, since the \(6\times6\) matrix \(A\) has six distinct eigenvalues, namely, the diagonal entries \(1,2,3,4,5,6\)
If an \(n\times n\) matrix has fewer than \(n\) distinct eigenvalues, must it fail to be diagonalizable?No. The projection in Example 1 has only the eigenvalues \(1\) and \(0\) and \(n=3\), and it is diagonalizable. Theorem 7.3.4 is a sufficient condition, not a necessary one.
Eigenvalues and Similarity
Theorem 7.3.5, Example 5, Exercises 35 and 36

If matrix \(A\) is similar to \(B\), what is the relationship between the eigenvalues of \(A\) and \(B\)?

Theorem 7.3.5: The eigenvalues of similar matrices

Suppose matrix \(A\) is similar to \(B\). Then

a. Matrices \(A\) and \(B\) have the same characteristic polynomial; that is, \(f_A(\lambda)=f_B(\lambda)\).

b. \(\operatorname{rank}A=\operatorname{rank}B\) and \(\operatorname{nullity}A=\operatorname{nullity}B\).

c. Matrices \(A\) and \(B\) have the same eigenvalues, with the same algebraic and geometric multiplicities. (However, the eigenvectors need not be the same.)

d. Matrices \(A\) and \(B\) have the same determinant and the same trace: \(\det A=\det B\) and \(\operatorname{tr}A=\operatorname{tr}B\).

Proof of a. If \(B=S^{-1}AS\), then \(f_B(\lambda)=\det(B-\lambda I_n)=\)\(\det(S^{-1}AS-\lambda I_n)=\det\big(S^{-1}(A-\lambda I_n)S\big)=(\det S)^{-1}\det(A-\lambda I_n)\det(S)=\det(A-\lambda I_n)=f_A(\lambda)\)

Example 5. Is the matrix \(A=\begin{bmatrix}2&3\\5&7\end{bmatrix}\) similar to \(B=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\8&5\end{bmatrix}\)?

Similar?No, since \(\operatorname{tr}A=9\) and \(\operatorname{tr}B=8\). See Theorem 7.3.5d.
Exercise 35. Is \(\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&3\end{bmatrix}\) similar to \(\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\1&2\end{bmatrix}\)?No: the traces are \(4\) and \(5\)
Exercise 36. Is \(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\5&3\end{bmatrix}\) similar to \(\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\4&3\end{bmatrix}\)?No: the traces are \(3\) and \(4\)
Algebraic versus Geometric Multiplicity; the Strategy
Theorems 7.3.6 and 7.3.7
Theorem 7.3.6: Algebraic versus geometric multiplicity

If \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of a square matrix \(A\), then

$$\operatorname{gemu}(\lambda)\le\operatorname{almu}(\lambda).$$
If \(\operatorname{almu}(\lambda)=1\), what is \(\operatorname{gemu}(\lambda)\)? (\(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue, so \(E_\lambda\ne\{\vec 0\}\).)\(1\): we have \(1\le\operatorname{gemu}(\lambda)\le\operatorname{almu}(\lambda)=1\)
Example 3: \(\operatorname{almu}(1)=2\), \(\operatorname{gemu}(1)=1\). Could \(\operatorname{gemu}(1)\) have been \(3\)?No, by Theorem 7.3.6. A \(3\times3\) matrix with the eigenvalue \(1\) of almu \(2\) has \(\operatorname{gemu}(1)\le2\).
Theorem 7.3.7: Strategy for diagonalization

Suppose we are asked to determine whether a given \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\) is diagonalizable. If so, we wish to find an invertible matrix \(S\) such that \(S^{-1}AS=B\) is diagonal. We can proceed as follows.

a. Find the eigenvalues of \(A\) by solving the characteristic equation \(f_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I_n)=0\).

b. For each eigenvalue \(\lambda\), find a basis of the eigenspace \(E_\lambda=\ker(A-\lambda I_n)\).

c. Matrix \(A\) is diagonalizable if (and only if) the dimensions of the eigenspaces add up to \(n\). In this case, we find an eigenbasis \(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_n\) for \(A\) by concatenating the bases of the eigenspaces we found in part b. Let

$$S=\begin{bmatrix}|&|&&|\\\vec v_1&\vec v_2&\cdots&\vec v_n\\|&|&&|\end{bmatrix}.\quad\text{Then}\quad S^{-1}AS=B=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1&0&\cdots&0\\0&\lambda_2&\cdots&0\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\0&0&\cdots&\lambda_n\end{bmatrix},$$

where \(\lambda_j\) is the eigenvalue associated with \(\vec v_j\).
