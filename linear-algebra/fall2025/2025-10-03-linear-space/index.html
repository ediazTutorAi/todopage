Introduction to Linear Spaces

MATH 3330 · Dr. Esteban Diaz
Motivation
Example 1: a differential equation

Consider the differential equation $$f''(x)+f(x)=0 .$$ A solution is a function \(f\) whose second derivative is \(-f\).

Two solutions you already know\(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\), since \((\sin x)''=-\sin x\) and \((\cos x)''=-\cos x\)
If \(f\) and \(g\) are solutions, is \(f+g\)?Yes: \((f+g)''+(f+g)=(f''+f)+(g''+g)=0\)
If \(f\) is a solution and \(k\) a constant, is \(kf\)?Yes: \((kf)''+kf=k(f''+f)=0\)
So every "linear combination" is a solution\(f(x)=c_1\sin x+c_2\cos x\). In fact all solutions have this form.

The solutions behave like vectors: we can add them and scale them. Their set looks like a 2-dimensional space with "basis" \(\sin x,\cos x\), but its elements are functions, not arrows or columns of numbers. This section makes that idea precise.

Definition 4.1.1
Linear spaces (or vector spaces)

A linear space \(V\) is a set with a rule for addition (if \(f,g\) are in \(V\), so is \(f+g\)) and a rule for scalar multiplication (if \(f\) is in \(V\) and \(k\in\mathbb{R}\), then \(kf\) is in \(V\)) satisfying these eight rules, for all \(f,g,h\) in \(V\) and all \(c,k\) in \(\mathbb{R}\):

  1. \((f+g)+h=f+(g+h)\)
  2. \(f+g=g+f\)
  3. There is a neutral element \(n\) in \(V\) with \(f+n=f\) for all \(f\). It is unique and denoted \(0\).
  4. For each \(f\) there is a \(g\) with \(f+g=0\). It is unique and denoted \(-f\).
  5. \(k(f+g)=kf+kg\)
  6. \((c+k)f=cf+kf\)
  7. \(c(kf)=(ck)f\)
  8. \(1f=f\)

In brief: a set with two reasonable operations, addition and scalar multiplication, that allow us to form linear combinations. Everything we did in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (span, independence, basis, dimension, linear transformations) only used these rules.

Definition 4.1.2
Subspaces, Examples 10, 12, 14

A subset \(W\) of a linear space \(V\) is a subspace of \(V\) if

  • a. \(W\) contains the neutral element \(0\) of \(V\);
  • b. \(W\) is closed under addition (if \(f,g\) are in \(W\), so is \(f+g\));
  • c. \(W\) is closed under scalar multiplication (if \(f\) is in \(W\) and \(k\) is a scalar, \(kf\) is in \(W\)).

We summarize b and c by saying that \(W\) is closed under linear combinations.

Ex. 10. The polynomials \(f(x)=a+bx+cx^2\) of degree \(\le2\) form a subspace of \(F(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})\). Check a, b, c:

a. Contains the zero function?\(f(x)=0=0+0x+0x^2\), yes
b. Closed under addition?\((a+bx+cx^2)+(p+qx+rx^2)=(a+p)+(b+q)x+(c+r)x^2\), still degree \(\le2\)
c. Closed under scalar multiplication?\(k(a+bx+cx^2)=ka+(kb)x+(kc)x^2\), yes

Ex. 12. Other subspaces of \(F(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})\): the differentiable functions, the smooth functions \(C^\infty\), all polynomials \(P\), and \(P_n\) (degree \(\le n\)). Ex. 14. The noninvertible \(2\times2\) matrices are not a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^{2\times2}\). Why? Not closed under addition: \(\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}=I_2\), and \(I_2\) is invertible.

Definition 4.1.3
Span, independence, basis, coordinates

Let \(f_1,\dots,f_n\) be elements of a linear space \(V\).

  • Span: every \(f\) in \(V\) is a linear combination \(c_1f_1+\cdots+c_nf_n\).
  • Linearly independent: \(c_1f_1+\cdots+c_nf_n=0\) has only the trivial solution \(c_1=\cdots=c_n=0\) (no \(f_i\) is redundant).
  • Basis: spans and independent. Then every \(f\) is a unique combination \(f=c_1f_1+\cdots+c_nf_n\).

The \(c_i\) are the coordinates of \(f\) with respect to \(\mathfrak{B}=(f_1,\dots,f_n)\), and \([f]_{\mathfrak{B}}=\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\ \vdots\\ c_n\end{bmatrix}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). The map \(L_{\mathfrak{B}}(f)=[f]_{\mathfrak{B}}\) is invertible with \(L_{\mathfrak{B}}^{-1}(\vec e_i)=f_i\).

Theorem 4.1.4. \(L_{\mathfrak{B}}\) is linear: \([f+g]_{\mathfrak{B}}=[f]_{\mathfrak{B}}+[g]_{\mathfrak{B}}\) and \([kf]_{\mathfrak{B}}=k[f]_{\mathfrak{B}}\). Why? Coordinates are the coefficients of the unique linear combination; adding or scaling combinations adds or scales coefficients.

Theorem 4.1.5
Dimension

If a linear space \(V\) has a basis with \(n\) elements, then all other bases of \(V\) consist of \(n\) elements as well. We say that \(n\) is the dimension of \(V\): $$\dim(V)=n .$$

Why? If \(\mathfrak{B}\) has \(n\) elements and \(\mathfrak{C}=(g_1,\dots,g_m)\) is another basis, then \([g_1]_{\mathfrak{B}},\dots,[g_m]_{\mathfrak{B}}\) are independent vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (by Theorem 4.1.4), so \(m\le n\). Swap the roles to get \(n\le m\).

So dimension is well defined, and \(V\) "looks like" \(\mathbb{R}^n\) through \(L_{\mathfrak{B}}\).

Summary 4.1.6
Finding a basis of a linear space \(V\), Example 17
  • a. Write down a typical element of \(V\), in terms of some arbitrary constants.
  • b. Using the arbitrary constants as coefficients, express your typical element as a linear combination of some elements of \(V\).
  • c. Verify that the elements of \(V\) in this linear combination are linearly independent. Then they form a basis of \(V\).

Example 17. Find a basis of the space \(V\) of all matrices \(B\) that commute with \(A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\2&3\end{bmatrix}\) (see Example 13).

Set \(B=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\) and impose \(BA=AB\)\(\begin{bmatrix}2b&a+3b\\2d&c+3d\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}c&d\\2a+3c&2b+3d\end{bmatrix}\), so \(2b=c,\ a+3b=d,\ 2d=2a+3c,\ c+3d=2b+3d\)
SolveThe last two equations are redundant: \(c=2b\), \(d=a+3b\), with \(a,b\) free
Typical element as a combination\(B=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\2b&a+3b\end{bmatrix}=a\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}+b\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\2&3\end{bmatrix}=aI_2+bA\)
Basis, dimension\(I_2\) and \(A\) are independent, so \((I_2,A)\) is a basis and \(\dim V=2\)
Theorem 4.1.7
Linear differential equations, Example 18

The solutions of the differential equation $$f''(x)+af'(x)+bf(x)=0\quad(a,b\text{ constants})$$ form a two-dimensional subspace of the space \(C^\infty\) of smooth functions. More generally, the solutions of $$f^{(n)}(x)+a_{n-1}f^{(n-1)}(x)+\cdots+a_1f'(x)+a_0f(x)=0$$ (\(a_0,\dots,a_{n-1}\) constants) form an \(n\)-dimensional subspace of \(C^\infty\). This is an \(n\)th-order linear differential equation with constant coefficients. (Proved in Section 9.3.)

Back to the motivation: \(f''+f=0\) is second order, so its solution space is 2-dimensional. Why is \((\cos x,\sin x)\) a basis? Both are solutions and they are independent. Two independent elements of a 2-dimensional space form a basis.

Example 18. Find all solutions of \(f''(x)+f'(x)-6f(x)=0\). Hint: try \(f(x)=e^{kx}\).

\(f(x)=e^{kx}\) is a solution if \(k^2e^{kx}+ke^{kx}-6e^{kx}=(k^2+k-6)e^{kx}=(k+3)(k-2)e^{kx}=0\), so \(k=2\) or \(k=-3\). Then \(e^{2x}\) and \(e^{-3x}\) are independent solutions, and by Theorem 4.1.7 the solution space is 2-dimensional, so they form a basis. All solutions: \(f(x)=c_1e^{2x}+c_2e^{-3x}\).
Definition 4.1.8
Finite dimensional spaces, Example 19

A linear space \(V\) is finite dimensional if it has a (finite) basis \(f_1,\dots,f_n\), so \(\dim(V)=n\). Otherwise it is infinite dimensional.

Example 19. Why can no finite list of polynomials span the space \(P\) of all polynomials?

Let \(N\) be the largest degree among \(f_1,\dots,f_n\). Every linear combination lies in \(P_N\), so a polynomial such as \(x^{N+1}\) is never in the span. Hence \(P\) is infinite dimensional (so are \(F(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})\) and the sequence space).
