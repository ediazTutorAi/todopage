Why Complex Numbers? Glucose and Insulin
The model that opens Section 7.5

Let \(g(t)\) be the excess glucose concentration in your blood and \(h(t)\) the excess insulin concentration, \(t\) in hours, measured between meals. One (linearized) model is $$g(t+1)=0.9\,g(t)-0.4\,h(t),\qquad h(t+1)=0.1\,g(t)+0.9\,h(t),$$ with initial values \(g(0)=100\) and \(h(0)=0\) after a heavy meal.

With \(\vec x(t)=\begin{bmatrix}g(t)\\h(t)\end{bmatrix}\), write \(\vec x(t+1)\) in terms of \(\vec x(t)\), and give a formula for \(\vec x(t)\).\(\vec x(t+1)=A\vec x(t)\) with \(A=\begin{bmatrix}0.9&-0.4\\0.1&0.9\end{bmatrix}\), so \(\vec x(t)=A^t\vec x(0)=A^t\begin{bmatrix}100\\0\end{bmatrix}\)
\(g(1)\) and \(h(1)\)\(g(1)=90\), \(h(1)=10\)
The characteristic polynomial \(f_A(\lambda)\)\(\lambda^2-1.8\lambda+0.85\)
The eigenvalues\(\lambda_{1,2}=\dfrac{1.8\pm\sqrt{3.24-3.4}}{2}=\dfrac{1.8\pm\sqrt{-0.16}}{2}=0.9\pm0.2i\). The square of a real number cannot be negative: there are no real eigenvalues. If we allow complex solutions, there are.

The trajectory spirals toward the origin: the glucose and insulin levels swing back and forth around the fasting level. This oscillatory behavior is what complex eigenvalues look like.

Complex Numbers: A Brief Review
Examples 1, 2 and 4
Complex numbers

A complex number can be expressed as \(z=a+ib\), where \(a\) and \(b\) are real numbers and \(i\cdot i=-1\). Then $$(a+ib)+(c+id)=(a+c)+i(b+d),\qquad(a+ib)(c+id)=(ac-bd)+i(ad+bc).$$ We call \(a\) the real part, \(\operatorname{Re}z\), and \(b\) the imaginary part, \(\operatorname{Im}z\). The complex number \(a+ib\) is represented by the vector \(\begin{bmatrix}a\\b\end{bmatrix}\) in the complex plane.

Example 1. Consider a nonzero complex number \(z\). What is the geometric relationship between \(z\) and \(iz\) in the complex plane?If \(z=a+ib\), then \(iz=-b+ia\). The vector \(\begin{bmatrix}-b\\a\end{bmatrix}\) is obtained by rotating \(\begin{bmatrix}a\\b\end{bmatrix}\) through \(90^\circ\) counterclockwise.
Conjugate, modulus, argument, polar form

The conjugate of \(z=a+ib\) is \(\bar z=a-ib\) (the reflection of \(z\) about the real axis). The length \(r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) of the vector is the modulus \(|z|\), and the polar angle \(\theta\) is an argument of \(z\), \(\arg z\) (determined only up to a multiple of \(2\pi\)). Then $$z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\qquad\text{(polar form)}.$$

Products in polar form

When we multiply two complex numbers, we multiply the moduli and add the arguments: $$|zw|=|z||w|,\qquad\arg(zw)=\arg z+\arg w\ \ (\text{modulo }2\pi).$$

Example 4. Describe the transformation \(T(z)=(3+4i)z\) from \(\mathbb C\) to \(\mathbb C\) geometrically.\(|T(z)|=|3+4i||z|=5|z|\) and \(\arg T(z)=\arctan\tfrac43+\arg z\approx53^\circ+\arg z\). \(T\) is a rotation through about \(53^\circ\) combined with a scaling by a factor of \(5\). Powers of Complex Numbers, and the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra
Theorems 7.5.1 and 7.5.2, Example 5
Theorem 7.5.1: De Moivre’s formula

$$(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n=\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta).$$ So if \(z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\), then \(z^n=r^n\big(\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta)\big)\): each multiplication by \(z\) multiplies the modulus by \(r\) and increases the argument by \(\theta\).

Example 5. Consider the complex number \(z=0.5+0.8i\). Represent the powers \(z^2,z^3,\dots\) in the complex plane. What is \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}z^n\)?\(r=\sqrt{0.5^2+0.8^2}\approx0.943\), \(\theta=\arctan\frac{0.8}{0.5}\approx58^\circ\). Each \(z^{n+1}\) is about \(5.7\%\) shorter than \(z^n\) and makes an angle of \(58^\circ\) with it: the tips spiral in toward the origin, and \(\lim z^n=0\) since \(r=|z|<1\).

Multiplication by \(z=0.5+0.8i\) has matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}0.5&-0.8\\0.8&0.5\end{bmatrix}\) with respect to the basis \(1,i\), and \(z^n\) is \(A^n\) applied to \(1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}\).

Exercise 9. Consider the complex number \(z=0.8-0.7i\). Represent the powers \(z^2,z^3,\dots\) in the complex plane and explain their long-term behavior.\(r=\sqrt{0.8^2+0.7^2}\approx1.063>1\), \(\theta\approx-41^\circ\). The tips spiral outward, clockwise, and \(|z^n|\to\infty\).

Theorem 7.5.2: Fundamental theorem of algebra

Any polynomial \(p(\lambda)\) with complex coefficients splits; that is, it can be written as a product of linear factors $$p(\lambda)=k(\lambda-\lambda_1)(\lambda-\lambda_2)\cdots(\lambda-\lambda_n),$$ for some complex numbers \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_n\) and \(k\). (The \(\lambda_i\) need not be distinct.) Therefore, a polynomial \(p(\lambda)\) of degree \(n\) has precisely \(n\) complex roots if they are properly counted with their multiplicities.

Split \(p(\lambda)=\lambda^2+1\), which has no real zeros.\(p(\lambda)=(\lambda+i)(\lambda-i)\)
For \(q(\lambda)=\lambda^2+b\lambda+c\) with \(b,c\) real, the complex roots are\(\lambda_{1,2}=\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4c}}{2}\), and \(q(\lambda)=(\lambda-\lambda_1)(\lambda-\lambda_2)\)
Exercise 11. Express the polynomial \(f(\lambda)=\lambda^3-3\lambda^2+7\lambda-5\) as a product of linear factors.\(f(1)=0\), and \(f(\lambda)=(\lambda-1)(\lambda^2-2\lambda+5)=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-1-2i)(\lambda-1+2i)\)
Exercise 12. Consider a polynomial \(f(\lambda)\) with real coefficients. Show that if a complex number \(\lambda_0\) is a root of \(f(\lambda)\), then so is its complex conjugate \(\bar\lambda_0\).Conjugation respects sums and products and fixes real numbers, so \(f(\bar\lambda_0)=\overline{f(\lambda_0)}=\bar0=0\). Complex roots of a real polynomial come in conjugate pairs.
Complex Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors
Linear algebra over \(\mathbb C\) and Example 6
Linear algebra over \(\mathbb C\)

Everything works the same with complex scalars, vectors with complex components and matrices with complex entries, except for the geometrical concepts defined in terms of the dot product (length, angles, orthogonality). Echelon form, linear transformation, kernel, image, linear independence, basis, dimension, coordinates, linear spaces, determinant, eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and diagonalization can all be generalized without difficulty.

Example 6. Diagonalize the rotation-scaling matrix \(A=\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{bmatrix}\) “over \(\mathbb C\).” Here, \(a\) and \(b\) are real numbers, and \(b\) is nonzero.

The characteristic polynomial \(f_A(\lambda)\)\(\det\begin{bmatrix}a-\lambda&-b\\b&a-\lambda\end{bmatrix}=(a-\lambda)^2+b^2\)
The eigenvalues: \((a-\lambda)^2=-b^2\), so\(a-\lambda=\pm ib\), that is, \(\lambda=a\pm ib\)
\(E_{a+ib}=\ker\begin{bmatrix}-ib&-b\\b&-ib\end{bmatrix}=\)\(\operatorname{span}\begin{bmatrix}i\\1\end{bmatrix}\)
\(E_{a-ib}=\ker\begin{bmatrix}ib&-b\\b&ib\end{bmatrix}=\)\(\operatorname{span}\begin{bmatrix}-i\\1\end{bmatrix}\)
Therefore, with \(R=\begin{bmatrix}i&-i\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\),\(R^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{bmatrix}R=\begin{bmatrix}a+ib&0\\0&a-ib\end{bmatrix}\)

Drag \(\vec v\) and watch \(A\vec v\) for the rotation-scaling matrices \(\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{bmatrix}\): \(A\vec v\) is never parallel to \(\vec v\) (no real eigenvector), yet the readout finds \(a\pm ib\).

In the readout, how do \(a\) and \(b\) give the rotation angle and the scaling factor of \(A\)?The scaling factor is \(|a+ib|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\), and the rotation angle is \(\arg(a+ib)\)

Example 7. Let \(A\) be a real \(2\times2\) matrix with eigenvalues \(a\pm ib\) (where \(b\neq0\)). Show that \(A\) is similar (over \(\mathbb R\)) to the matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{bmatrix}\), representing a rotation combined with a scaling.

Theorem 7.5.3: Complex eigenvalues and rotation-scaling matrices

If \(A\) is a real \(2\times2\) matrix with eigenvalues \(a\pm ib\) (where \(b\neq0\)), and if \(\vec v+i\vec w\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(a+ib\), then $$S^{-1}AS=\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{bmatrix},\qquad\text{where }S=\begin{bmatrix}\vec w&\vec v\end{bmatrix}.$$

Let \(\vec v\pm i\vec w\) be eigenvectors with eigenvalues \(a\pm ib\). Then \(P^{-1}AP=\)\(\begin{bmatrix}a+ib&0\\0&a-ib\end{bmatrix}\), where \(P=\begin{bmatrix}\vec v+i\vec w&\vec v-i\vec w\end{bmatrix}\)
By Example 6 the rotation-scaling matrix is similar to the same diagonal matrix, via \(R\). Then \(\begin{bmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{bmatrix}=S^{-1}AS\) with \(S=PR^{-1}\). Compute \(S\).\(S=PR^{-1}=\dfrac1{2i}\begin{bmatrix}\vec v+i\vec w&\vec v-i\vec w\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&i\\-1&i\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\vec w&\vec v\end{bmatrix}\), which has real entries, as claimed

Counting Eigenvalues, Trace and Determinant
Theorems 7.5.4 and 7.5.5, Example 9
Theorem 7.5.4

A complex \(n\times n\) matrix has \(n\) complex eigenvalues if they are counted with their algebraic multiplicities.

By the fundamental theorem of algebra, the characteristic polynomial always splits: \(f_A(\lambda)=(\lambda_1-\lambda)(\lambda_2-\lambda)\cdots(\lambda_n-\lambda)\).

Example 9. Consider an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\) with complex eigenvalues \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_n\), listed with their algebraic multiplicities. What is the relationship between the \(\lambda_i\) and the determinant of \(A\)? Compare with Theorem 7.2.8.\(f_A(0)=\det A=\lambda_1\lambda_2\cdots\lambda_n\), so \(\det A=\lambda_1\lambda_2\cdots\lambda_n\)
Theorem 7.5.5: Trace, determinant, and eigenvalues

Consider an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\) with complex eigenvalues \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_n\), listed with their algebraic multiplicities. Then $$\operatorname{tr}A=\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\cdots+\lambda_n\qquad\text{and}\qquad\det A=\lambda_1\lambda_2\cdots\lambda_n.$$

Check Theorem 7.5.5 on the glucose matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}0.9&-0.4\\0.1&0.9\end{bmatrix}\), \(\lambda=0.9\pm0.2i\).\(\operatorname{tr}A=1.8=(0.9+0.2i)+(0.9-0.2i)\), \(\det A=0.81+0.04=0.85=(0.9+0.2i)(0.9-0.2i)\)
27. Suppose a real \(3\times3\) matrix \(A\) has only two distinct eigenvalues. Suppose that \(\operatorname{tr}A=1\) and \(\det A=3\). Find the eigenvalues of \(A\) with their algebraic multiplicities.Complex eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs, so with only two distinct eigenvalues both are real: \(a,a,b\). Then \(2a+b=1\) and \(a^2b=3\), so \(2a^3-a^2+3=(a+1)(2a^2-3a+3)=0\). The quadratic has no real roots, so \(a=-1\), \(b=3\): eigenvalues \(-1\) (algebraic multiplicity \(2\)) and \(3\).
28. Suppose a \(3\times3\) matrix \(A\) has the real eigenvalue \(2\) and two complex eigenvalues. Also, suppose that \(\det A=50\) and \(\operatorname{tr}A=8\). Find the complex eigenvalues of \(A\).The complex pair is \(p\pm iq\): \(2+2p=8\) gives \(p=3\), and \(2(p^2+q^2)=50\) gives \(q=4\). The eigenvalues are \(3\pm4i\).
30a. If \(2i\) is an eigenvalue of a real \(2\times2\) matrix \(A\), find \(A^2\).The eigenvalues are \(\pm2i\), so \(f_A(\lambda)=\lambda^2+4\) and, by the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, \(A^2+4I_2=0\): \(A^2=-4I_2\)
30b. Give an example of a real \(2\times2\) matrix \(A\) such that all the entries of \(A\) are nonzero and \(2i\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\).We need \(\operatorname{tr}A=0\) and \(\det A=4\): for example \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&-5\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\), with \(f_A=\lambda^2+4\)
