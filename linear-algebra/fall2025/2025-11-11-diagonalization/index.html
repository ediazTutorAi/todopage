Diagonalizable Matrices
Definition 7.1.1
Definition 7.1.1: Diagonalizable matrices

Consider a linear transformation \(T(\vec x)=A\vec x\) from \(\mathbb R^n\) to \(\mathbb R^n\). Then \(A\) (or \(T\)) is said to be diagonalizable if the matrix \(B\) of \(T\) with respect to some basis is diagonal.

By Theorem 3.4.4 and Definition 3.4.5, matrix \(A\) is diagonalizable if (and only if) \(A\) is similar to some diagonal matrix \(B\), meaning that there exists an invertible matrix \(S\) such that \(S^{-1}AS=B\) is diagonal.

To diagonalize a square matrix \(A\) means to find an invertible matrix \(S\) and a diagonal matrix \(B\) such that \(S^{-1}AS=B\).

Consider a basis \(\mathfrak B=(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_n)\) of \(\mathbb R^n\). The \(\mathfrak B\)-matrix \(B\) of \(T\) is diagonal if (and only if) \(A\vec v_1=\lambda_1\vec v_1,\dots,A\vec v_n=\lambda_n\vec v_n\) for some scalars \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n\).

$$B=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1&0&\cdots&0\\0&\lambda_2&\cdots&0\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\0&0&\cdots&\lambda_n\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{with columns }\ [A\vec v_1]_{\mathfrak B},\ [A\vec v_2]_{\mathfrak B},\dots$$
The first column of \(B\) is \([A\vec v_1]_{\mathfrak B}=\)\([\lambda_1\vec v_1]_{\mathfrak B}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1\\0\\\vdots\\0\end{bmatrix}\)
We can conclude that an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\) is diagonalizable if (and only if)there exists a basis \(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_n\) of \(\mathbb R^n\) such that \(A\vec v_1=\lambda_1\vec v_1,\dots,A\vec v_n=\lambda_n\vec v_n\) for some scalars \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n\)
Examples 9 and 10 of Section 3.4: the reflection about a line in \(\mathbb R^2\) isdiagonalizable
and the rotation \(T(\vec x)=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\vec x\) isnot diagonalizable
Eigenvectors, Eigenvalues, and Eigenbases
Definition 7.1.2
Definition 7.1.2: Eigenvectors, eigenvalues, and eigenbases

Consider a linear transformation \(T(\vec x)=A\vec x\) from \(\mathbb R^n\) to \(\mathbb R^n\).

A nonzero vector \(\vec v\) in \(\mathbb R^n\) is called an eigenvector of \(A\) (or \(T\)) if

$$A\vec v=\lambda\vec v$$

for some scalar \(\lambda\). This \(\lambda\) is called the eigenvalue associated with eigenvector \(\vec v\).

A basis \(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_n\) of \(\mathbb R^n\) is called an eigenbasis for \(A\) (or \(T\)) if the vectors \(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_n\) are eigenvectors of \(A\), meaning that \(A\vec v_1=\lambda_1\vec v_1,\dots,A\vec v_n=\lambda_n\vec v_n\) for some scalars \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n\).

A nonzero vector \(\vec v\) in \(\mathbb R^n\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) if \(A\vec v\) is parallel to \(\vec v\). Drag \(\vec v\) and watch \(A\vec v\).

If \(\vec v\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\), then \(A^2\vec v=\)\(A(\lambda\vec v)=\lambda(A\vec v)=\lambda^2\vec v\)
and in general, for all positive integers \(m\), \(A^m\vec v=\)\(\lambda^m\vec v\). So \(\vec v\) is an eigenvector of \(A^2,A^3,\dots\) as well.
Proof by induction: \(A^{m+1}\vec v=A(A^m\vec v)=\)\(A(\lambda^m\vec v)=\lambda^m(A\vec v)=\lambda^{m+1}\vec v\)
Eigenbases and Diagonalization
Theorem 7.1.3
Theorem 7.1.3: Eigenbases and diagonalization

The matrix \(A\) is diagonalizable if (and only if) there exists an eigenbasis for \(A\).

If \(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_n\) is an eigenbasis for \(A\), with \(A\vec v_1=\lambda_1\vec v_1,\dots,A\vec v_n=\lambda_n\vec v_n\), then the matrices

$$S=\begin{bmatrix}|&|&&|\\\vec v_1&\vec v_2&\cdots&\vec v_n\\|&|&&|\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad B=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1&0&\cdots&0\\0&\lambda_2&\cdots&0\\\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\0&0&\cdots&\lambda_n\end{bmatrix}$$

will diagonalize \(A\), meaning that \(S^{-1}AS=B\).

Conversely, if the matrices \(S\) and \(B\) diagonalize \(A\), then the column vectors of \(S\) will form an eigenbasis for \(A\), and the diagonal entries of \(B\) will be the associated eigenvalues.

Proof of one implication, by matrix products. Suppose there exists an eigenbasis \(\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_n\) for \(A\), and define \(S\) and \(B\) as in the theorem.

\(AS=A[\vec v_1\ \vec v_2\ \cdots\ \vec v_n]=\)\([A\vec v_1\ A\vec v_2\ \cdots\ A\vec v_n]=[\lambda_1\vec v_1\ \lambda_2\vec v_2\ \cdots\ \lambda_n\vec v_n]\)
and this equals\([\vec v_1\ \vec v_2\ \cdots\ \vec v_n]\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)=SB\)
Therefore\(AS=SB\), so \(S^{-1}AS=B\), as claimed. (The converse is Exercise 23.)

Examples 2, 3 and 4

Example 2. Find all eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the identity matrix \(I_n\). Is there an eigenbasis for \(I_n\)? Is \(I_n\) diagonalizable?

\(I_n\vec v=\vec v=1\vec v\) for all \(\vec v\) in \(\mathbb R^n\), soall nonzero vectors in \(\mathbb R^n\) are eigenvectors of \(I_n\), with eigenvalue \(1\); all bases of \(\mathbb R^n\) are eigenbases
Is \(I_n\) diagonalizable?Yes, it is diagonal already. If \(S\) is any invertible matrix, then \(S^{-1}I_nS=I_n\) is diagonal.

Eigenvalues of Orthogonal Matrices; Invertibility
Example 5, Theorem 7.1.4, Example 6, Summary 7.1.5

Example 5. What are the possible real eigenvalues of an orthogonal \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\)?

\(T(\vec x)=A\vec x\) preserves length: \(\|T(\vec x)\|=\|A\vec x\|=\)\(\|\vec x\|\) for all \(\vec x\) in \(\mathbb R^n\) (Definition 5.3.1)
For an eigenvector \(\vec v\) with \(A\vec v=\lambda\vec v\): \(\|\vec v\|=\|A\vec v\|=\|\lambda\vec v\|=\)\(|\lambda|\,\|\vec v\|\), so \(|\lambda|=1\) and \(\lambda=1\) or \(\lambda=-1\)
For example, the reflection \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}\) has\(A\vec e_1=1\vec e_1\) and \(A\vec e_2=-\vec e_2=(-1)\vec e_2\), so \(1\) and \(-1\) are the eigenvalues of \(A\)
Theorem 7.1.4

The possible real eigenvalues of an orthogonal matrix are \(1\) and \(-1\).

Example 6. For which \(n\times n\) matrices \(A\) is \(0\) an eigenvalue? Give your answer in terms of the kernel of \(A\) and also in terms of the invertibility of \(A\).

\(0\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) if there exists a nonzero \(\vec v\) with \(A\vec v=0\vec v=\vec 0\), that is,a nonzero vector in the kernel of \(A\). Thus \(0\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) if (and only if) \(\ker A\ne\{\vec 0\}\), meaning that \(A\) fails to be invertible.
Summary 7.1.5: Various characterizations of invertible matrices

For an \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\), the following statements are equivalent:

i. \(A\) is invertible.   ii. The linear system \(A\vec x=\vec b\) has a unique solution \(\vec x\), for all \(\vec b\) in \(\mathbb R^n\).   iii. \(\operatorname{rref}A=I_n\).   iv. \(\operatorname{rank}A=n\).   v. \(\operatorname{im}A=\mathbb R^n\).   vi. \(\ker A=\{\vec 0\}\).   vii. The column vectors of \(A\) form a basis of \(\mathbb R^n\).   viii. The column vectors of \(A\) span \(\mathbb R^n\).   ix. The column vectors of \(A\) are linearly independent.   x. \(\det A\ne0\).   xi. \(0\) fails to be an eigenvalue of \(A\).
