The Law of Sines

Area of a Triangle
Combining area of triangles and right triangle trigonometry

We can develop the Law of Sines by combining two familiar topics, area of triangles and right triangle trigonometry.

Example 1. Use the familiar formula for area of a triangle, \(\text{Area} = \dfrac{1}{2}(\text{base})(\text{height})\), along with right triangle trigonometry, to verify each of the area rules given below.

(a) \(\text{Area} = \dfrac{1}{2}bc\sin(A)\)
\(\sin(A) = \dfrac{h}{c}\), so \(h = c\sin(A)\)
\(\text{Area} = \dfrac{1}{2}(b)(c\sin(A)) = \dfrac{1}{2}bc\sin(A)\)
(b) \(\text{Area} = \dfrac{1}{2}ab\sin(C)\)
\(\sin(C) = \dfrac{h}{a}\), so \(h = a\sin(C)\)
\(\text{Area} = \dfrac{1}{2}(b)(a\sin(C)) = \dfrac{1}{2}ab\sin(C)\)
Theorem: Area of a Triangle
THEOREM: Area of a Triangle

For any triangle \(ABC\), with side measures \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\), the area is given by any of the following formulas.

\[\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}bc\sin(A) = \frac{1}{2}ac\sin(B) = \frac{1}{2}ab\sin(C)\]

While the above result provides a useful means for calculating the area of a triangle, perhaps more importantly, it establishes a valuable equivalence relationship. The Law of Sines is a direct consequence of the above statement.

Applying the Law of Sines
Example 2
THEOREM: Law of Sines

For any triangle \(ABC\), with side measures \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\), we have the following relationship.

\[\frac{\sin(A)}{a} = \frac{\sin(B)}{b} = \frac{\sin(C)}{c}\]

Alternatively, we can restate the above as follows:

\[\frac{a}{\sin(A)} = \frac{b}{\sin(B)} = \frac{c}{\sin(C)}\]

Example 2. Show that the law of sines is a direct consequence of the area formulas established earlier.

Multiply by 2:\(bc\sin(A) = ac\sin(B) = ab\sin(C)\)
Divide by \(abc\):\(\dfrac{\sin(A)}{a} = \dfrac{\sin(B)}{b} = \dfrac{\sin(C)}{c}\)
Example 3
Ship and lighthouse

A ship sailing due east spots a lighthouse 19 km away located at a bearing of \(N\,40^\circ 20'\,E\). Thirty minutes later, the lighthouse is located at a bearing of \(N\,20^\circ 35'\,W\). How fast is the ship sailing in km/hr?

We know\(A = 90^\circ - 40^\circ 20' = 49^\circ 40'\), \(C = 90^\circ - 20^\circ 35' = 69^\circ 25'\), \(c = 19\)
We need\(b\)
Third angle\(B = 180^\circ - 49^\circ 40' - 69^\circ 25' = 60^\circ 55'\)
Law of Sines\(\dfrac{b}{\sin(B)} = \dfrac{c}{\sin(C)}\), \(b = \sin\left(60^\circ 55'\right)\cdot\dfrac{19}{\sin\left(69^\circ 25'\right)} \approx 17.74\) km
Speed\(b\) is the distance in half an hour: \(2 \times 17.74 \approx 35.47\) km/hr
Example 4
Solve the triangle \(ABC\) given \(A = 32.2^\circ\), \(B = 52.4^\circ\) and \(b = 42\) cm
We know\(A = 32.2^\circ\), \(B = 52.4^\circ\), \(b = 42\)
We need\(C\), \(c\), \(a\)
Angle sum\(C = 180^\circ - 32.2^\circ - 52.4^\circ = 95.4^\circ\)
Find \(c\)\(\dfrac{c}{\sin(C)} = \dfrac{b}{\sin(B)}\), \(c = \sin(95.4^\circ)\cdot\dfrac{42}{\sin(52.4^\circ)} \approx 52.78\) cm
Find \(a\)\(\dfrac{a}{\sin(A)} = \dfrac{b}{\sin(B)}\), \(a = \sin(32.2^\circ)\cdot\dfrac{42}{\sin(52.4^\circ)} \approx 28.25\) cm
The Ambiguous Case of the Law of Sines
One angle and two sides

You may have noticed in each of the preceding examples we were provided with two angle measures and one side measure. With this information, we can quickly determine the third missing angle and apply the law of sines to solve for all remaining side measures. However, we must exercise caution when only one angle measure is provided. In particular, with only one angle and two side measures (one of which must be opposite the given angle to apply the law of sines), we may arrive at one of three possible outcomes, (1) a single unique triangle exists, (2) two triangles exist, and (3) no such triangle exists.

THEOREM: The Longest Side Theorem

If one side of a triangle is longer than another side, then the angle opposite the longer side is the larger angle.

Conversely, if one angle of a triangle is larger than another angle, then the side opposite the larger angle is the longer side.

On your own, apply the above theorem to our last example to verify our conclusion that no such triangle exists satisfying the given information.

\(a = 52.4 > b = 32.2\), so \(A > B = 95^\circ\). Then \(A + B > 190^\circ > 180^\circ\), which is impossible.

Example 6
Solve the triangle \(ABC\) given \(a = 18.2\) cm, \(b = 22.5\) cm, and \(B = 65^\circ\)
We know\(a = 18.2\), \(b = 22.5\), \(B = 65^\circ\)
Find \(A\)\(\sin(A) = \dfrac{a\sin(B)}{b}\), \(A = \sin^{-1}\left(\dfrac{18.2\sin(65^\circ)}{22.5}\right) \approx 47.15^\circ\). One of two possibilities! Recall \(\sin(\theta) = \sin(180^\circ - \theta)\): \(A_2 = 180^\circ - 47.15^\circ = 132.85^\circ\)
Triangle 1 (\(A_1 \approx 47.15^\circ\))\(C_1 = 180^\circ - 65^\circ - 47.15^\circ \approx 67.85^\circ\)
\(\dfrac{c_1}{\sin(C_1)} = \dfrac{b}{\sin(B)}\), \(c_1 = \sin(67.85^\circ)\cdot\dfrac{22.5}{\sin(65^\circ)} \approx 22.99\) cm
Triangle 2 (\(A_2 \approx 132.85^\circ\))\(C_2 = 180^\circ - 65^\circ - 132.85^\circ \approx -17.85^\circ\), not possible
ConclusionOnly one triangle.
Example 7
Solve the triangle \(ABC\) given \(c = 32.5\) cm, \(b = 29.7\) cm, and \(B = 65^\circ\)
We know\(b = 29.7\), \(c = 32.5\), \(B = 65^\circ\)
Find \(C\)\(\sin(C) = \dfrac{c\sin(B)}{b}\), \(C = \sin^{-1}\left(\dfrac{32.5\sin(65^\circ)}{29.7}\right) \approx 82.64^\circ\). One of two possibilities! \(C_2 = 180^\circ - 82.64^\circ = 97.36^\circ\)
Triangle 1 (\(C_1 \approx 82.64^\circ\))\(A_1 = 180^\circ - 65^\circ - 82.64^\circ \approx 32.36^\circ\)
\(a_1 = \sin(32.36^\circ)\cdot\dfrac{29.7}{\sin(65^\circ)} \approx 17.54\) cm
Triangle 2 (\(C_2 \approx 97.36^\circ\))\(A_2 = 180^\circ - 65^\circ - 97.36^\circ \approx 17.64^\circ\)
\(a_2 = \sin(17.64^\circ)\cdot\dfrac{29.7}{\sin(65^\circ)} \approx 9.93\) cm
ConclusionTwo possible triangles!
