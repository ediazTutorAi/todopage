Applications of Right Triangles
Calculator Approximations

When dealing with applications involving trigonometric functions, it's quite rare that we can determine exact values the way we have so far. In these cases, we'll use a calculator's numerical capabilities to determine reasonable approximations.

Most calculators evaluate sine, cosine, and tangent directly. Secant, cosecant, and cotangent aren't usually built in, so we first rewrite them as reciprocals: \(\sec(x) = \dfrac{1}{\cos(x)}\), \(\csc(x) = \dfrac{1}{\sin(x)}\), \(\cot(x) = \dfrac{1}{\tan(x)}\).

Example 1
Approximate each expression, to the nearest hundredth

(a) \(\sin(23°16'22'')\)

Convert to decimal degrees first: \(23° + \dfrac{16}{60}° + \dfrac{22}{3600}° \approx 23.2728°\)

\(\sin(23.2728°) \approx 0.40\)

(b) \(\sec(-95.25°)\)

\(\dfrac{1}{\cos(-95.25°)} \approx -10.93\)

(c) \(\cot(45')\)

45 arcminutes \(= \dfrac{45}{60}° = 0.75°\)

\(\dfrac{1}{\tan(0.75°)} \approx 76.39\)

Inverse Trigonometric Functions
Restricted to acute angles, for now

Inverse trig functions can get involved in general, so for now we'll only consider them as they apply to acute angles in a right triangle. In general, given a function \(f\) and its inverse \(f^{-1}\), if \(f(a) = b\), then \(a = f^{-1}(b)\), the inverse form of the original statement.

\(\sin(x) = y\)↔\(\sin^{-1}(y) = x\)
\(\cos(x) = y\)↔\(\cos^{-1}(y) = x\)
\(\tan(x) = y\)↔\(\tan^{-1}(y) = x\)

One helpful way to think about the statements above: \(x\) is an angle measure and \(y\) is a real number representing the function's value at that angle.

Example 2 (a)–(b)
Write the inverse form, then solve

Assume \(x\) is an acute angle, in degrees. Give exact answers when possible.

(a) \(\sin(x) = \dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\)

A special-angle ratio, recognizable from the QI reference chart.

\(\sin^{-1}\left(\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right) = x \ \Rightarrow\ x = 45°\)

(b) \(\sec(x) = \dfrac{7}{4}\)

Rewrite in terms of cosine first: \(\cos(x) = \dfrac{4}{7}\)

\(\cos^{-1}\left(\dfrac{4}{7}\right) = x \ \Rightarrow\ x \approx 55.15°\)

Example 2 (c)–(d)
Write the inverse form, then solve

(c) \(\cos^{-1}(y) = 60°\)

A special angle again, recognizable from the QI reference chart.

\(\cos(60°) = y \ \Rightarrow\ y = \dfrac{1}{2}\)

(d) \(\tan^{-1}(y) = 32.235°\)

\(\tan(32.235°) = y \ \Rightarrow\ y \approx 0.63\)

Solving Right Triangles
Finding every angle and every side

To solve a triangle means to find the measures of all angles and sides. For a triangle \(ABC\), it's customary to label the side opposite angle \(A\) as side \(a\), the side opposite angle \(B\) as side \(b\), and so on. In a right triangle, we'll often place the right angle at \(C\), with the hypotenuse as side \(c\).

The unlabeled right-angle vertex is \(C\); side \(c\) (the hypotenuse) is always opposite it.

Example 3 (a)
Wheelchair ramp: the maximum incline angle

Building codes often regulate the steepness of wheelchair access ramps. Suppose a code requires a maximum vertical rise of \(1\) unit for every \(10\) units of horizontal distance. What's the largest possible incline angle \(\theta\) for the ramp?

By TOA, \(\tan(\theta) = \dfrac{\text{opp}}{\text{adj}} = \dfrac{1}{10}\), so \(\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{10}\right) \approx 5.7°\)

Example 3 (b)
Is there enough room for a code-compliant ramp?

You've been contracted to build the ramp. It must start at a horizontal distance of \(25\) feet from the door's landing, and the landing sits \(2\) feet above the sidewalk. Is there enough space to satisfy the code from Example 3(a)?

The \(1{:}10\) ratio means a \(2\) ft rise requires at least \(2 \times 10 = 20\) ft of horizontal run. \(25\) ft is available, which is more than enough (\(5\) ft to spare)

Example 3 (c)
Design plans: solve the ramp's triangle

Sketch a right triangle for the ramp design and record every angle and side measure, for the city engineers' permit application.

\(c \approx 25.08\) ft

Known: \(a = 2\) ft, \(b = 25\) ft, \(C = 90°\).

Find \(c\) (Pythagorean theorem): \(c^2 = 25^2 + 2^2 = 629 \ \Rightarrow\ c = \sqrt{629} \approx 25.08\) ft

Find \(A\): \(\tan(A) = \dfrac{2}{25} \ \Rightarrow\ A = \tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{2}{25}\right) \approx 4.57°\)

Find \(B\) (using the given legs directly): \(\tan(B) = \dfrac{25}{2} \ \Rightarrow\ B = \tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{25}{2}\right) \approx 85.43°\)

Check: \(A + B + C = 4.57° + 85.43° + 90° = 180°\) ✓

Angle of Elevation and Angle of Depression
Definitions

Any angle measured from the horizontal is called an angle of elevation: the positive acute angle between the horizontal and the line of sight to an object above it. An object below the horizontal produces a negative angle of elevation instead.

A positive acute angle measured from the horizontal downward is called an angle of depression, the magnitude of a negative angle of elevation. We'll restrict "angle of elevation" to positive acute angles only.

This terminology comes from surveying: before GPS and lasers, a surveyor could estimate elevation with nothing more than a simple clinometer.

Example 4
Surveying a mountain's elevation

A surveyor works from a small, flat valley with known elevation \(4500\) ft. Using a clinometer, he measures the angle of elevation to the summit as \(35.8°\). After walking \(1000\) ft directly toward the mountain, he measures it again: \(39.9°\). Find the elevation at the peak.

(Valley elevation: \(4500\) ft. Not to scale.)

Let \(x\) be the remaining horizontal distance from the second measurement to a point directly below the peak, and \(h\) the peak's height above the valley floor. Both sightlines share the same \(h\):

\(\tan(35.8°) = \dfrac{h}{1000+x} \ \Rightarrow\ h = (1000+x)\tan(35.8°)\)
\(\tan(39.9°) = \dfrac{h}{x} \ \Rightarrow\ h = x\tan(39.9°)\)

Setting these equal and solving for \(x\):

\(x\tan(39.9°) = 1000\tan(35.8°) + x\tan(35.8°)\)
\(x\big(\tan(39.9°) - \tan(35.8°)\big) = 1000\tan(35.8°)\)
\(x = \dfrac{1000\tan(35.8°)}{\tan(39.9°)-\tan(35.8°)} \approx 6276.58 \text{ ft}\)

\(h \approx 6276.58 \cdot \tan(39.9°) \approx 5248.03\) ft, so the peak's elevation is \(4500 + 5248 = \mathbf{9748}\) ft.

Looking Ahead
Beyond right triangles

Every technique here (calculator approximations, inverse trig functions, angle of elevation/depression) relies on the triangle having a right angle, so SOH-CAH-TOA applies directly. Many real triangles don't have one. Solving those general triangles needs new tools entirely: the Law of Sines and the Law of Cosines.
