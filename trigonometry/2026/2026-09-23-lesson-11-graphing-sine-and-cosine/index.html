Graphing Sine and Cosine
Circular versus rectangular graphs

Thus far, we've considered a strictly circular graph for the sine and cosine functions: the unit circle. In this lesson we'll transition to the more familiar rectangular perspective typically associated with graphing. To do this, we'll treat our angle \(\theta\) as the input, graphed along the horizontal axis, with the corresponding output graphed along the vertical axis, exactly as usual.

Every point on the sine and cosine graphs to come is read directly off a point like this one: the angle \(\theta\) gives the position around the circle, and the \(y\)-coordinate (for sine) or \(x\)-coordinate (for cosine) becomes the graph's output.

Graph of the Sine Function
Angles along the horizontal axis, \(y\)-values along the vertical

Graph the sine function by plotting angles from the unit circle along the horizontal axis, and the corresponding output (the \(y\)-values from the unit circle) along the vertical axis.

Drag the point around the circle on the left — the point tracing the curve on the right follows it live, at exactly that angle's \(y\)-value.

Each cycle has exactly \(5\) points of interest — the \(y\)-intercept, each \(x\)-intercept, and the peak and valley in between — which partition the period into \(4\) equal subintervals. Watch for that same \(4\)-way split later in this lesson, when sketching a curve from its amplitude and period alone.

Important Info: Amplitude

When graphing trigonometric functions, amplitude is a term that applies only to sine and cosine, referring to the rise and fall the curve makes above and below its own horizontal (sinusoidal) axis.

For \(y = a\sin x\) or \(y = a\cos x\), the amplitude is \(|a|\) — that is, amplitude is always a positive value. If \(a < 0\), the graph is reflected across the sinusoidal axis, but the amplitude itself doesn't change sign.

Drag the slider to change \(a\) in \(y = a\sin x\) — the circle's own radius and the curve rescale together, live. Try dragging \(a\) past \(0\) to see the reflection the paragraph above describes.

Example 1 (a)–(b)
Determine the amplitude

(a) \(y = 3\sin(2x) - 1\)

amp \(= |3| = 3\)

(b) \(y = \dfrac{1}{2} - 2\cos(\pi + 3x)\)

amp \(= |{-2}| = 2\)

Example 2
Graph \(y = -\dfrac{3}{2}\cos x\) and \(y = \cos x\)

Graph \(y = -\dfrac{3}{2}\cos x\) and \(y = \cos x\) on the same coordinate axes.

Drag the point around the circle — it traces \(y = -\dfrac{3}{2}\cos x\) live, alongside the plain \(y = \cos x\) reference curve.

period \(= 2\pi\), step \(= \dfrac{\text{period}}{4} = \dfrac{2\pi}{4} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}\), amp \(= \left|-\dfrac{3}{2}\right| = \dfrac{3}{2}\) (reflected across the horizontal axis, since \(a < 0\))

Important Info: Period

Functions like sine and cosine are cyclic, with a definite repeating pattern — these are called periodic functions, and period is the term we'll use to describe this behavior: the horizontal length it takes to complete one full cycle.

For \(y = \sin(bx)\) or \(y = \cos(bx)\), with \(b > 0\), the period is equal to \(\dfrac{2\pi}{b}\).

Drag the slider to change \(b\) in \(y = \sin(bx)\) — watch the period shrink as \(b\) grows and stretch as \(b\) shrinks, live.

A few values of \(b\) worth checking by hand against the slider above: \(b=1\) gives period \(2\pi\); \(b=2\) gives period \(\dfrac{2\pi}{2} = \pi\); \(b = \dfrac{1}{2}\) gives period \(\dfrac{2\pi}{1/2} = 4\pi\).

Example 3 (a)–(b)
Determine the period

(a) \(y = 3\sin(2x) - 1\)

period \(= \dfrac{2\pi}{2} = \pi\)

(b) \(y = \dfrac{1}{2} - 2\cos(\pi + 3x)\)

period \(= \dfrac{2\pi}{3}\)

Example 4
Graph \(y = \sin(3x)\) and \(y = \sin(x)\)

Graph \(y = \sin(3x)\) and \(y = \sin(x)\) on the same coordinate axes.

Drag the point around the circle — it traces \(y = \sin(3x)\) live, alongside the plain \(y = \sin x\) reference curve. Notice it completes a full cycle three times as fast.

\(y = \sin(x)\): period \(= 2\pi\), step \(= \dfrac{2\pi}{4} = \dfrac{\pi}{2} = \dfrac{3\pi}{6}\) (common denominator).   \(y = \sin(3x)\): period \(= \dfrac{2\pi}{3}\), step \(= \dfrac{2\pi/3}{4} = \dfrac{2\pi}{12} = \dfrac{\pi}{6}\)

Example 5 (a)
Identify amplitude and period, then graph

(a) \(y = -3\sin(4x)\)

period \(= \dfrac{2\pi}{4} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}\), step \(= \dfrac{\pi/2}{4} = \dfrac{\pi}{8}\), amp \(= |{-3}| = 3\) (reflected across the horizontal axis)

Example 5 (b)
Identify amplitude and period, then graph

(b) \(f(\theta) = 5\cos\left(\dfrac{\theta}{2}\right)\)

period \(= \dfrac{2\pi}{1/2} = 4\pi\), step \(= \dfrac{4\pi}{4} = \pi\), amp \(= |5| = 5\)

Looking Ahead
From the parent curves to full transformations

Every graph in this lesson was built from just two numbers: amplitude (the vertical stretch, from the multiplier out front) and period (the horizontal stretch, from the multiplier on \(x\)). The same \(4\)-way split of one period into quarter-period steps is exactly how a curve gets sketched by hand, without plotting point by point. From here, the next natural step is combining amplitude and period changes with horizontal and vertical shifts — and eventually graphing the other four trig functions the same way.
