Applications Involving Radian Measure
Arc length, angular/linear speed, and sector area

Now that angles can be measured in radians, three closely related quantities follow almost for free: the length of the arc a central angle intercepts, the speed of a point traveling around a circle, and the area swept out by a central angle. All three show up constantly outside the classroom: flight distances along the globe, gear ratios in a transmission, the spin rate of a hard disk, even the shape of a set of stairs.

As in the last lesson, we will lean on ratio and proportion rather than memorized formulas wherever possible. The formulas fall out of the same proportion each time.

Important Info: Arc Length

Given a circle with radius \(r\) and a central angle \(\theta\), in radians, the length of the intercepted arc, denoted \(s\), is given by

\(s = r\theta\),   \(\theta\) in radians

As an alternative to a memorized rule, arc length can be found with ratio and proportion instead. A central angle \(\theta\) is some part of a full rotation, so

\(\dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}} = \dfrac{\theta}{360°}\) (\(\theta\) in degrees), or equivalently \(\dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}} = \dfrac{\theta}{2\pi}\) (\(\theta\) in radians)

and the arc it intercepts is the same part of the full circumference:

\(\dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}} = \dfrac{s}{2\pi r}\)

Setting these equal gives the working proportion for every arc-length problem:

\(\dfrac{s}{2\pi r} = \dfrac{\theta}{360°}\) (\(\theta\) in degrees), or equivalently \(\dfrac{s}{2\pi r} = \dfrac{\theta}{2\pi}\) (\(\theta\) in radians)

Example 1
Verify \(s = r\theta\) using ratio and proportion

(a) Given an angle \(\theta\) in radians, use ratio and proportion to verify \(s = r\theta\).

\(\dfrac{\theta}{2\pi} = \dfrac{s}{2\pi r} \;\Rightarrow\; s = 2\pi r\left(\dfrac{\theta}{2\pi}\right) = r\theta\),   \(\theta\) in radians

(b) Develop a similar statement for \(\theta\) in degrees.

\(\dfrac{\theta}{360°} = \dfrac{s}{2\pi r} \;\Rightarrow\; s = 2\pi r\left(\dfrac{\theta}{360°}\right) = \dfrac{\pi}{180}r\theta\),   \(\theta\) in degrees

This is exactly why the "no units means radians" convention from Lesson 08 matters here: \(s = r\theta\) is only correct when \(\theta\) is in radians. Plugging in a degree measure without the \(\pi/180\) conversion gives the wrong arc length.

Example 2
Arc length on a circle of diameter 6 in.

Consider a circle with a diameter of \(6\) inches (so \(r = 3\) in). Find the length of the arc intercepted by each central angle \(\theta\).

(a) \(\theta = \dfrac{4\pi}{3}\)

\(\dfrac{\theta}{2\pi} = \dfrac{s}{2\pi r} \;\Rightarrow\; s = 2\pi(3)\left(\dfrac{4\pi/3}{2\pi}\right) = 4\pi\) in.

(b) \(\theta = 225°\)

\(\dfrac{\theta}{360°} = \dfrac{s}{2\pi r} \;\Rightarrow\; s = 2\pi(3)\left(\dfrac{225}{360}\right) = \dfrac{1350}{360}\pi = \dfrac{15}{4}\pi\) in.

Example 3
DFW to the equator

In an earlier lesson, we were given the Earth's circumference to be approximately \(25{,}000\) miles, with DFW International Airport located at approximately \(32°53'59"\text{N}\), \(97°2'25"\text{W}\). Roughly how far is a direct route flight from DFW to the Earth's equator? (Source: Google Maps, maps.google.com)

Latitude is the central angle \(\theta\) between the equator (the \(0°\) reference) and DFW's ray from the Earth's center, so the flight distance is just the arc that angle intercepts.

\(32°53'59" = \left(32 + \dfrac{53}{60} + \dfrac{59}{3600}\right)° \approx 32.8997°\). \(\dfrac{\theta}{360°} = \dfrac{s}{25000} \;\Rightarrow\; s \approx 25000\left(\dfrac{32.8997}{360}\right) \approx 2284.70\) mi.

Explore: Gear Ratios
Two meshed gears, live

One direct application of arc length is gearing. Every mechanical system built around rotation, including a vehicle's transmission, relies on gear ratios: for a \(3.00\!:\!1\) input:output ratio, three rotations of the input gear (from the engine) turn the output gear (connected to the driveshaft) once. Two meshed gears share exactly one thing: the arc length that passes between their teeth at the point of contact.

Drag either gear's blue spoke, or type new radii above the diagram to try a different pair of gears entirely. These start matching Example 4 below: input radius \(2\) in., output radius \(3\) in. Notice the shared arc length readout stays consistent no matter which gear you turn, since that shared arc length is exactly what links their rotations, whatever the radii are.

Example 4 (a)
Gearing

An input gear of radius \(2\) in. drives an output gear of radius \(3\) in. (a) How much must the input gear rotate to drive a one-quarter turn on the output gear?

A \(\tfrac{1}{4}\) turn of the output gear sweeps arc length \(s = \tfrac{1}{4}(2\pi \cdot 3) = \tfrac{3\pi}{2}\) in. That same arc length \(s = \tfrac{3\pi}{2}\) in. must pass along the input gear's own circumference, so \(\dfrac{\theta}{360°} = \dfrac{s}{2\pi r} \;\Rightarrow\; \theta = 360°\left(\dfrac{3\pi/2}{2\pi(2)}\right) = 360°\left(\dfrac{3}{8}\right) = 135°\).

Example 4 (b)
Gearing, input:output ratio

(b) What is the input:output ratio for this configuration?

\(\dfrac{135°}{360°} : \dfrac{90°}{360°} \;\Rightarrow\; 135 : 90 \;\Rightarrow\; 1.5 : 1\). Every \(1.5\) rotations of the input gear drives \(1\) rotation on the output gear.

Important Info: Linear and Angular Speed

Consider a circle centered at the origin, with a central angle \(\theta\) increasing at a constant rate. Let \(P\) be the point along the circle intercepted by the terminal side of \(\theta\).

The rate of change in the position of \(P\) along the circle is the linear speed, \(v\):

\(v = \dfrac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} = \dfrac{s}{t}\)

The rate of change in the angle measure is the angular speed, \(\omega\) (omega):

\(\omega = \dfrac{\text{amt. rotation}}{\text{time}} = \dfrac{\theta}{t}\),   \(\theta\) in radians

\(\omega = \theta/t\) only applies to \(\theta\) in radians, and it's uncommon enough that, just like arc length, ratio and proportion will get us further than memorizing it.

Watch it happen at an actual constant rate: press Play and \(P\) sweeps the circle below at \(\pi/5\) rad/sec (the same rate Example 5 uses), or type a different rate above the diagram first. Both \(\theta/t\) and \(s/t\) keep re-settling on whatever that rate is, no matter how long \(t\) and \(\theta\) themselves have kept climbing.

Example 5 (a)
A point moving around a circle

Consider a circle with radius \(10\) cm and central angle \(\theta\). Let \(P\) be the point intercepted by the terminal side of \(\theta\), with \(\theta\) increasing at a constant rate of \(\pi/5\) radians per second.

(a) What angle is formed by \(P\) after \(1\) second, \(5\) seconds, and \(8\) seconds?

\(\left(\dfrac{\pi}{5} \tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{sec}}\right)(1\text{ sec}) = \dfrac{\pi}{5}\) rad,   \(\left(\dfrac{\pi}{5} \tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{sec}}\right)(5\text{ sec}) = \pi\) rad,   \(\left(\dfrac{\pi}{5} \tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{sec}}\right)(8\text{ sec}) = \dfrac{8\pi}{5}\) rad

Example 5 (b)–(c)
Distance traveled and linear speed

(b) Find the distance traveled by \(P\) in \(8\) seconds.

\(\dfrac{8\pi/5}{2\pi} = \dfrac{s}{2\pi(10)} \;\Rightarrow\; s = 2\pi(10)\left(\dfrac{8\pi/5}{2\pi}\right) = \dfrac{80}{5}\pi = 16\pi \approx 50.27\) cm.

(c) Find the linear speed at which \(P\) is moving, in centimeters per minute.

Convert rad/sec directly to cm/sec (rotational \(\to\) linear) using \(s = r\theta\), then sec \(\to\) min: \(\dfrac{\pi}{5}\left(\tfrac{\text{rad}}{\text{sec}}\right) (10\text{ cm})\left(\tfrac{60\text{ sec}}{1\text{ min}}\right) = \dfrac{\pi}{5}(10)(60) = 120\pi\) cm/min.

Example 6 (a)
A rotating wheel, angular speed

A wheel with a radius of \(13\) inches is rotating at \(2\) revolutions per second.

(a) Find the angular speed for this wheel, in radians per second, degrees per second, and rotations per minute.

\(\dfrac{2\text{ rev}}{1\text{ sec}}\left(\dfrac{2\pi\text{ rad}}{1\text{ rev}}\right) = 4\pi\) rad/sec    \(\dfrac{2\text{ rev}}{1\text{ sec}}\left(\dfrac{360°}{1\text{ rev}}\right) = 720°\)/sec    \(\dfrac{2\text{ rev}}{1\text{ sec}}\left(\dfrac{1\text{ rot}}{1\text{ rev}}\right)\left(\dfrac{60\text{ sec}}{1\text{ min}}\right) = 120\) rot/min

Example 6 (b)
A rotating wheel, linear speed

(b) Find the linear speed for the wheel in miles per hour.

\(\dfrac{2\text{ rev}}{1\text{ sec}}\left(\dfrac{2\pi(13)\text{ in}}{1\text{ rev}}\right) \left(\dfrac{1\text{ ft}}{12\text{ in}}\right)\left(\dfrac{1\text{ mi}}{5280\text{ ft}}\right) \left(\dfrac{60\text{ sec}}{1\text{ min}}\right)\left(\dfrac{60\text{ min}}{1\text{ hr}}\right) = \dfrac{2(2\pi \cdot 13)(3600)}{12(5280)} = \dfrac{187200\pi}{63360} = \dfrac{65\pi}{22} \approx 9.28\) mph.

Example 7
A hard disk drive

The hard disk for a computer has a diameter of \(3.5\) inches and a spin rate of \(5400\) RPM (rotations per minute).

(a) What is the angular velocity of the hard disk, in radians per second?

\(\dfrac{5400\text{ rot}}{1\text{ min}}\left(\dfrac{2\pi\text{ rad}}{1\text{ rot}}\right)\left(\dfrac{1\text{ min}}{60\text{ sec}}\right) = \dfrac{5400(2\pi)}{60} = 180\pi\) rad/sec.

(b) If the read/write head of the disk drive is located \(1\) inch from the axis of rotation, what is the linear speed of the disk below it?

\(\dfrac{5400\text{ rot}}{1\text{ min}}\left(\dfrac{2\pi(1)\text{ in}}{1\text{ rot}}\right) = 10800\pi\) in/min.

Note the diameter (\(3.5\) in.) was extra information here: the read/write head sits \(1\) in. from the axis, not at the disk's outer edge, so that's the radius that matters for this part.

Important Info: Area of a Sector

Given a circle with radius \(r\) and a central angle \(\theta\) in radians, the area of a sector \(A\) is given by

\(A = \dfrac{1}{2}r^2\theta\),   \(\theta\) in radians

As with arc length, the same ratio-and-proportion idea works here: a sector's area is the same part of the whole circle's area that \(\theta\) is of a full rotation.

\(\dfrac{A}{\pi r^2} = \dfrac{\theta}{360°}\) (\(\theta\) in degrees), or equivalently \(\dfrac{A}{\pi r^2} = \dfrac{\theta}{2\pi}\) (\(\theta\) in radians)

Example 8
Revolving doors and a step

A building has revolving doors at a right-angled corner entry. The area swept out by the doors is \(16\pi\) square feet, and the main landing has an area of \(117\pi\) square feet. If city code requires steps to have a minimum tread depth of \(12\) inches (\(1\) ft), what is the total surface area of the step leading to the landing?

The landing and the doors' sweep together form one quarter-circle, of some radius \(r_1\), centered at the corner. The step is a quarter-circle ring (an "annulus sector") one tread-depth further out, from \(r_1\) to \(r_2 = r_1 + 1\) ft.

Find \(r_1\), the landing's outer radius. One quarter of the doors' full circle sweep is \(\tfrac{1}{4}(16\pi) = 4\pi\) ft², so the landing plus doors together cover \(117\pi + 4\pi = 121\pi\) ft², a quarter circle of radius \(r_1\):

\(\dfrac{121\pi}{\pi r_1^2} = \dfrac{90°}{360°} \;\Rightarrow\; r_1^2 = 4(121) = 484 \;\Rightarrow\; r_1 = 22\) ft.

Find the step's area. The tread depth pushes the outer edge to \(r_2 = 22 + 1 = 23\) ft, so the step is the quarter-circle ring between \(r_1 = 22\) and \(r_2 = 23\):

\(\dfrac{1}{4}\pi r_2^2 - \dfrac{1}{4}\pi r_1^2 = \dfrac{1}{4}\pi(23)^2 - \dfrac{1}{4}\pi(22)^2 = \dfrac{529\pi}{4} - \dfrac{484\pi}{4} = \dfrac{45\pi}{4} \approx 35.34\) ft².

Looking Ahead
One relationship, three quantities

Arc length, angular/linear speed, and sector area all came from the same single proportion: a central angle is the same fraction of a full rotation as its arc, its area, or (dividing by time) its speed are of the circle's own circumference, area, or rotation rate. The unit circle, coming up next, is what happens when this idea is pinned to a circle of radius exactly \(1\): arc length and angle become numerically identical, which is exactly why \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) works as a coordinate pair.
