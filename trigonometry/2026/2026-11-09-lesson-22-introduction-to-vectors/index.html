Introduction to Vectors
Important Terminology
DEFINITION: Scalar and Vector Quantities, Resultants, Equilibrants, and the Zero Vector

Quantities that can be described by magnitude alone, for example, speed, mass, temperature, etc., are called scalar quantities.

Quantities that have both magnitude and direction, such as velocity, acceleration, etc., are called vector quantities.

Vector quantities are often represented by directed line segments called vectors. The length of this vector denotes the magnitude of the vector quantity, while the orientation indicates the direction of the vector quantity.

A vector can be named by two points. The first point indicates the initial point or the tail, and the second point indicates the terminal point or the head.

The magnitude (or length) of any vector \(\mathbf{v}\) is denoted as \(|\mathbf{v}|\).

When two vectors have the same magnitude and direction, they are said to be equal.

Vectors can be added together. The sum of any two vectors is called the resultant, which is also a vector.

For any vector \(\mathbf{v}\), a vector with equal magnitude but in the opposing direction is called an equilibrant and is denoted as \(-\mathbf{v}\).

The sum of any vector \(\mathbf{v}\), and its equilibrant \(-\mathbf{v}\), is called the zero vector, denoted \(\mathbf{0}\) (a vector quantity), and has a magnitude of \(0\) (a scalar quantity).

One common vector topic is that of force. In general, whenever we engage in an action that has the potential to accelerate an object, for example, pushing or pulling, we experience force.

Parallelograms and Vectors
Parallelograms are often used to visualize and understand vector operations, such as addition and subtraction.
IMPORTANT INFO: Parallelograms and the Resultant

Given two vectors, \(\mathbf{v}\) and \(\mathbf{w}\), we can construct a parallelogram by arranging these vectors as opposite sides.

We visualize the resultant, \(\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}\), as the directed diagonal formed with the initial side at the common initial point, point \(P\), and terminal side at the common terminal point, point \(Q\).

Note: In a parallelogram, opposite sides are parallel and opposite angles are congruent. Additionally, we can conclude that adjacent angles in a parallelogram are supplementary. (Hint: From our parallel lines and transversals discussion, these angles are same-side interior.)
