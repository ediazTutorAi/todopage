Vector Fields
Definition
Definition

A vector field \(\vec F\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) is an assignment of a two-dimensional vector \(\vec F(x,y)\) to each point \((x,y)\) of a subset \(D\) of \(\mathbb{R}^2\). The subset \(D\) is the domain of the vector field.

A vector field \(\vec F\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is an assignment of a three-dimensional vector \(\vec F(x,y,z)\) to each point \((x,y,z)\) of a subset \(D\) of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). The subset \(D\) is the domain of the vector field.

A vector field in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) can be represented in either of two equivalent ways:

$$\vec F(x,y)=\langle P(x,y),Q(x,y)\rangle,\qquad \vec F(x,y)=P(x,y)\,\mathbf i+Q(x,y)\,\mathbf j.$$

A vector field is said to be continuous if its component functions are continuous.

Radial Fields
Example 6.2 and Checkpoint 6.2
Radial field

In a radial field, all vectors either point directly toward or directly away from the origin. Furthermore, the magnitude of any vector depends only on its distance from the origin.

The vector located at point \((x,y)\) is perpendicular to the circle centered at the origin that contains point \((x,y)\), and all other vectors on this circle have the same magnitude.

Rotational Fields
Examples 6.3, 6.4, 6.5 and Checkpoints 6.3, 6.4
Rotational field

In a rotational field, the vector at point \((x,y)\) is tangent (not perpendicular) to a circle with radius \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). In a standard rotational field, all vectors point either in a clockwise direction or in a counterclockwise direction, and the magnitude of a vector depends only on its distance from the origin.

A: Example 6.3, \(\vec F=\langle y,-x\rangle\).   B: Checkpoint 6.3, \(\vec F=\langle-2y,2x\rangle\).   W: Example 6.4, \(\vec F=\dfrac{y}{x^2+y^2}\mathbf i-\dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2}\mathbf j\).

Example 6.3. Describe \(\vec F(a,b)=\langle b,-a\rangle\)It points clockwise and is perpendicular to the radial vector \(\langle a,b\rangle\): \(\langle a,b\rangle\cdot\langle-b,a\rangle=-ab+ab=0\). It has length \(r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\). So the vector at \((a,b)\) has length \(r\), is tangent to the circle of radius \(r\), and points clockwise.
Example 6.4. \(\|\vec F(a,b)\|\) and the domain\(\|\vec F(a,b)\|=\sqrt{\dfrac{a^2+b^2}{(a^2+b^2)^2}}=\sqrt{\dfrac1{a^2+b^2}}\to\infty\) as \((a,b)\to(0,0)\). The domain is all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) except \((0,0)\). It models the whirlpool motion of the river.
Checkpoint 6.3. Is \(\langle-2y,2x\rangle\) radial, rotational, or neither?Rotational (counterclockwise).
Example 6.5. \(\vec v=-\dfrac{2y}{x^2+y^2}\mathbf i+\dfrac{2x}{x^2+y^2}\mathbf j\) is the velocity field of a fluid. How fast is it moving at \((1,-1)\), in m/s?\(\vec v(1,-1)=\mathbf i+\mathbf j\), so the speed is \(\|\mathbf i+\mathbf j\|=\sqrt2\) m/s.
Checkpoint 6.4. \(\vec v(x,y)=\langle4|x|,1\rangle\) models the velocity of water on the surface of a river. Speed at \((2,3)\), in m/s?\(\vec v(2,3)=\langle8,1\rangle\), so the speed is \(\sqrt{65}\) m/s.
Unit Vector Fields
Example 6.6 and Checkpoint 6.5
Unit vector field

A vector field \(\vec F\) is a unit vector field if the magnitude of each vector in the field is \(1\). In a unit vector field, the only relevant information is the direction of each vector.

If \(\vec F=\langle P,Q,R\rangle\) is a vector field, then the corresponding unit vector field is \(\Big\langle\dfrac{P}{\|\vec F\|},\dfrac{Q}{\|\vec F\|},\dfrac{R}{\|\vec F\|}\Big\rangle\). The process of dividing \(\vec F\) by its magnitude to form the unit vector field \(\vec F/\|\vec F\|\) is called normalizing the field \(\vec F\).

Example 6.6. Show that \(\vec G(x,y)=\Big\langle\dfrac{y}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}},-\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\Big\rangle\) is a unit vector field.\(\sqrt{\dfrac{y^2}{x^2+y^2}+\dfrac{x^2}{x^2+y^2}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{x^2+y^2}{x^2+y^2}}=1\). Therefore \(\vec G\) is a unit vector field.
Checkpoint 6.5. Is \(\vec F(x,y)=\langle-y,x\rangle\) a unit vector field?No: \(\|\vec F\|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\).
The unit vector field corresponding to \(\vec F(x,y)=\langle y,-x\rangle\) (Example 6.3)\(\|\vec F\|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\), so it is the field \(\vec G\) of Example 6.6.
Vector Fields in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)
Examples 6.7, 6.8 and Checkpoints 6.6, 6.7

A vector field in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is written \(\vec F(x,y,z)=\langle P(x,y,z),Q(x,y,z),R(x,y,z)\rangle\) or \(\vec F(x,y,z)=P\,\mathbf i+Q\,\mathbf j+R\,\mathbf k\).

Example 6.7. Describe \(\vec F(x,y,z)=\langle1,1,z\rangle\).The \(x\) and \(y\) components are constant. In the \(xy\)-plane (\(z=0\)) each vector is \(\langle1,1,0\rangle\). Slightly above the plane the vector points slightly upward; far above, the \(z\) component is large and the vector is almost vertical.
Checkpoint 6.6. Sketch \(\vec G(x,y,z)=\langle2,\dfrac z2,1\rangle\).At \((a,b,0)\): \(\langle2,0,1\rangle\). At height \(z\): \(\langle2,\tfrac z2,1\rangle\), the \(y\) component growing linearly with \(z\).

Example 6.8. Newton's law of gravitation states that \(\vec F=-G\dfrac{m_1m_2}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}\), where \(G\) is the universal gravitational constant. It describes the gravitational field exerted by an object (object 1) of mass \(m_1\) located at the origin on another object (object 2) of mass \(m_2\) located at \((x,y,z)\). The minus sign shows that the force is attractive.

$$\vec F=-G\,m_1m_2\Big\langle\frac{x}{r^3},\frac{y}{r^3},\frac{z}{r^3}\Big\rangle,\qquad r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}.$$

This is an example of a radial vector field in \(\mathbb{R}^3\). The magnitudes of the vectors increase as the vectors get closer to the origin.

Checkpoint 6.7. Asteroid 1 (\(750{,}000\) kg) is at the origin and asteroid 2 (\(130{,}000\) kg) is at \((15,-5,10)\), in units of \(10^8\) km. With \(G=6.67384\times10^{-11}\ \text{m}^3\text{kg}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}\), find the force vector that asteroid 1 exerts on asteroid 2.In meters: \((1.5\times10^{12},\,-5\times10^{11},\,10^{12})\), \(r\approx1.87\times10^{12}\) m, \(Gm_1m_2\approx6.51\). So \(\vec F\approx\langle-1.49\times10^{-24},\ 4.97\times10^{-25},\ -9.94\times10^{-25}\rangle\) N.
Gradient Fields
Example 6.9 and Checkpoint 6.8

If \(f\) is a scalar function of \(x\) and \(y\), the gradient is \(\nabla f=f_x(x,y)\,\mathbf i+f_y(x,y)\,\mathbf j\), a vector field in \(\mathbb{R}^2\). For \(f(x,y,z)\), \(\nabla f=f_x\,\mathbf i+f_y\,\mathbf j+f_z\,\mathbf k\), a vector field in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Definition: gradient field (conservative field)

A vector field \(\vec F\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) or in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) is a gradient field if there exists a scalar function \(f\) such that \(\nabla f=\vec F\). In this situation, \(f\) is called a potential function for \(\vec F\).

Example 6.9: use technology to plot the gradient vector field of \(f(x,y)=x^2y^2\). The curves are level curves of \(f\).

The gradient vectors are perpendicular to the level curves, and the magnitudes of the vectors get larger as the level curves get closer together.

Example 6.9. The gradient of \(f(x,y)=x^2y^2\)\(\nabla f=\langle2xy^2,\ 2x^2y\rangle\)
Checkpoint 6.8. The gradient vector field of \(f(x,y)=\sin x\cos y\)\(\nabla f=\langle\cos x\cos y,\ -\sin x\sin y\rangle\)

In some applications (certain contexts in physics) a potential function is defined instead by \(-\nabla f=\vec F\).

Uniqueness of Potential Functions
Theorem 6.1, Examples 6.10, 6.11 and Checkpoints 6.9, 6.10

To be connected means: if \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) are any two points in the domain, then you can walk from \(P_1\) to \(P_2\) along a path that stays entirely inside the domain.

Theorem 6.1: Uniqueness of Potential Functions

Let \(\vec F\) be a conservative vector field on an open and connected domain and let \(f\) and \(g\) be functions such that \(\nabla f=\vec F\) and \(\nabla g=\vec F\). Then, there is a constant \(C\) such that \(f=g+C\).

Example 6.10. Is \(f(x,y,z)=x^2yz-\sin(xy)\) a potential function for \(\vec F(x,y,z)=\langle2xyz-y\cos(xy),\ x^2z-x\cos(xy),\ x^2y\rangle\)?\(f_x=2xyz-y\cos(xy)\), \(f_y=x^2z-x\cos(xy)\), \(f_z=x^2y\). Therefore \(\nabla f=\vec F\) and \(f\) is a potential function for \(\vec F\).
Checkpoint 6.9. Is \(f(x,y,z)=x^2\cos(yz)+y^2z^2\) a potential function for \(\vec F=\langle2x\cos(yz),\ -x^2z\sin(yz)+2yz^2,\ y^2\rangle\)?No: \(f_z=-x^2y\sin(yz)+2y^2z\neq y^2\), so \(\nabla f\neq\vec F\).
Example 6.11. The velocity of a fluid is \(\vec v(x,y)=\langle xy,\ \dfrac{x^2}2-y\rangle\). Verify that \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2y}2-\dfrac{y^2}2\) is a potential function for \(\vec v\).\(f_x=xy\) and \(f_y=\dfrac{x^2}2-y\). Therefore \(\nabla f=\vec v\).
Checkpoint 6.10. Verify that \(f(x,y)=x^3y^2+1\) is a potential function for \(\vec v(x,y)=\langle3x^2y^2,\ 2x^3y\rangle\).\(f_x=3x^2y^2\) and \(f_y=2x^3y\). Therefore \(\nabla f=\vec v\).
Cross-Partial Property
Theorem 6.2, Examples 6.12, 6.13 and Checkpoint 6.12
Theorem 6.2: The Cross-Partial Property of Conservative Vector Fields

Let \(\vec F\) be a vector field in two or three dimensions such that the component functions of \(\vec F\) have continuous first-order partial derivatives on the domain of \(\vec F\).

If \(\vec F(x,y)=\langle P(x,y),Q(x,y)\rangle\) is a conservative vector field in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), then \(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}=\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}\).

If \(\vec F(x,y,z)=\langle P,Q,R\rangle\) is a conservative vector field in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), then \(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}=\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x},\ \dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial z}=\dfrac{\partial R}{\partial y},\ \text{and}\ \dfrac{\partial R}{\partial x}=\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial z}\).

A: \(\langle y,-x\rangle\) (Example 6.12).   B: \(\langle x^2y,\ \tfrac{x^3}{3}\rangle\).

Example 6.12. Show that the rotational vector field \(\vec F(x,y)=\langle y,-x\rangle\) is not conservative.Let \(P=y\), \(Q=-x\). If \(\vec F\) is conservative then \(P_y=Q_x\). But \(P_y=1\) and \(Q_x=-1\), so the vector field is not conservative.
Example 6.13. Is \(\vec F(x,y,z)=\langle7,-2,x^3\rangle\) conservative?\(P=7\), \(Q=-2\), \(R=x^3\). \(P_y=Q_x=R_y=Q_z=0\), so the first two equalities hold. But \(R_x=3x^2\) and \(P_z=0\), so \(\vec F\) is not conservative.
Checkpoint 6.12. Is \(\vec G(x,y,z)=\langle y,x,xyz\rangle\) conservative?\(R_y=xz\) but \(Q_z=0\). Not conservative.

The theorem can only help determine that a field is not conservative; it does not let you conclude that a vector field is conservative. For example, \(\vec F(x,y)=\langle x^2y,\tfrac{x^3}{3}\rangle\) has the cross-partial property, but that alone does not show it is conservative. We learn later how to conclude that \(\vec F\) is conservative.

Flow Lines
Exercises 30, 31
Flow line

A flow line (or streamline) of a vector field \(\vec F\) is a curve \(\vec r(t)\) such that \(d\vec r/dt=\vec F(\vec r(t))\).

If \(\vec F\) represents the velocity field of a moving particle, then the flow lines are paths taken by the particle. Therefore, flow lines are tangent to the vector field.

Exercise 30. Show that \(\vec c(t)=\langle e^{2t},\ \ln|t|,\ \tfrac1t\rangle\), \(t\neq0\), is a flow line of \(\vec F(x,y,z)=\langle2x,\ z,\ -z^2\rangle\).\(\vec{c}'(t)=\langle2e^{2t},\ \tfrac1t,\ -\tfrac1{t^2}\rangle=\langle2x,\ z,\ -z^2\rangle\) at \((x,y,z)=\vec c(t)\).
Exercise 31. Show that \(\vec c(t)=\langle\sin t,\ \cos t,\ e^t\rangle\) is a flow line of \(\vec F(x,y,z)=\langle y,\ -x,\ z\rangle\).\(\vec{c}'(t)=\langle\cos t,\ -\sin t,\ e^t\rangle=\langle y,\ -x,\ z\rangle\) at \(\vec c(t)\).

Electric Field
Exercises 28, 29

An electric field in the \(xy\)-plane caused by an infinite line of charge along the \(x\)-axis is a gradient field with potential function

$$V(x,y)=c\,\ln\!\Big(\frac{r_0}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\Big),$$

where \(c>0\) is a constant and \(r_0\) is a reference distance at which the potential is assumed to be zero.

Exercise 28. Find the components of the electric field \(\vec E(x,y)=-\nabla V(x,y)\).\(\vec E=\Big\langle\dfrac{c\,x}{x^2+y^2},\ \dfrac{c\,y}{x^2+y^2}\Big\rangle\)
Exercise 29. Show that the electric field is directed outward from the origin and has magnitude \(E=\dfrac cr\), \(r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\).\(\vec E=\dfrac{c}{x^2+y^2}\langle x,y\rangle\) is a positive multiple of \(\langle x,y\rangle\), so it points outward, and \(\|\vec E\|=\dfrac{c\,r}{r^2}=\dfrac cr\).
