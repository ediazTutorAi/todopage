Lagrange Multipliers

Optimization with a Constraint
Why we need a new method

In the max/min lesson we hunted for the highest or lowest point of \(f(x,y)\) anywhere. Real problems rarely allow that. A company has a fixed budget. A fence has a fixed length. A point must stay on a given curve. We still want the best value of \(f\), but only among the points that obey a rule.

We write the rule as a constraint \(g(x,y)=0\):

$$\text{maximize or minimize } f(x,y) \quad\text{subject to}\quad g(x,y)=0.$$

What does "subject to \(g(x,y)=0\)" do to the search, geometrically?

It shrinks the list of candidates from a whole region of the plane to the points on one curve. We are no longer searching across the plane, only along the curve \(g=0\).

One option is to solve the constraint for one variable and substitute, which works for easy constraints. Lagrange's method is for when that is messy, and it also explains why the answer looks the way it does.

Theorem 4.20 and Why It Works
Method of Lagrange Multipliers: One Constraint

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be functions of two variables with continuous partial derivatives at every point of some open set containing the smooth curve \(g(x,y)=0\). Suppose \(f\), restricted to points on that curve, has a local extremum at \((x_0,y_0)\), and \(\nabla g(x_0,y_0)\neq \mathbf 0\). Then there is a number \(\lambda\), called the Lagrange multiplier, for which

$$\nabla f(x_0,y_0)=\lambda\,\nabla g(x_0,y_0).$$

What is the geometric meaning of "\(\nabla f\) is a multiple of \(\nabla g\)"?

The two gradient vectors point along the same line (same or opposite direction). The number \(\lambda\) says how many times longer \(\nabla f\) is, and a negative \(\lambda\) means opposite directions.

A gradient \(\nabla f\) is perpendicular to what?the contour (level curve) of \(f\) through that point
\(\nabla g\) is perpendicular to what?the constraint curve \(g=0\) itself, since that curve is the contour \(g=0\)
At a constrained extremum, how do the contour of \(f\) and the constraint curve meet?They touch and share a tangent line. If the contour crossed the curve, we could slide along the curve to a bigger or smaller contour value, so \(f\) would not be at an extremum.
So what can we say about \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\)?Both are perpendicular to the same tangent line, so they are parallel: \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\).
Steps for Using Lagrange Multipliers
Problem-solving strategy
  1. Identify the objective function \(f(x,y)\) and the constraint \(g(x,y)=0\). Are we maximizing or minimizing?
  2. Set up the system $$\nabla f(x_0,y_0)=\lambda\nabla g(x_0,y_0),\qquad g(x_0,y_0)=0.$$
  3. Solve for \(x_0\), \(y_0\), and \(\lambda\).
  4. Compare the values of \(f\) at every solution. The largest value is the maximum, the smallest is the minimum.

Two unknowns plus \(\lambda\) is three unknowns, and the system has three equations: one for each component of the gradient equation, plus the constraint.

Example 4.42
Minimize \(f(x,y)=x^2+4y^2-2x+8y\) subject to \(x+2y=7\)
Constraint function\(g(x,y)=x+2y-7=0\)
\(\nabla f\)\(\langle 2x-2,\; 8y+8\rangle\)
\(\nabla g\)\(\langle 1,\; 2\rangle\)
The system\(2x_0-2=\lambda,\quad 8y_0+8=2\lambda,\quad x_0+2y_0-7=0\)
Eliminate \(\lambda\)\(8y_0+8=2(2x_0-2)\) gives \(x_0=2y_0+3\)
Use the constraint\((2y_0+3)+2y_0=7\), so \(y_0=1\) and \(x_0=5\). Also \(\lambda=8\).
Minimum value\(f(5,1)=25+4-10+8=\mathbf{27}\)
Sanity check\(f(7,0)=35\) and \(f(0,3.5)=77\) are larger, and the strip graph bottoms out at \(27\).
Example 4.43
Golf ball production: maximize \(z=48x+96y-x^2-2xy-9y^2\) subject to \(20x+4y=216\)

Profit is in thousands of dollars, \(x\) and \(y\) are production levels of two kinds of ball, and the constraint is the budget. Divide the budget equation by \(4\) to simplify it.

Constraint function\(g(x,y)=5x+y-54=0\)
\(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\)\(\nabla f=\langle 48-2x-2y,\; 96-2x-18y\rangle\), \(\nabla g=\langle 5,\,1\rangle\)
The system\(48-2x_0-2y_0=5\lambda,\quad 96-2x_0-18y_0=\lambda,\quad 5x_0+y_0-54=0\)
Eliminate \(\lambda\)Substitute the second into the first: \(x_0=54-11y_0\)
Use the constraint\(5(54-11y_0)+y_0=54\) gives \(y_0=4\), so \(x_0=10\)
Maximum profit\(f(10,4)=480+384-100-80-144=540\), that is \$540,000
Endpoint check\(f(10.8,0)=401.76\) and \(f(0,54)=-21{,}060\), both smaller
Three Variables and Two Constraints
Example 4.44, then the two-multiplier system

Nothing changes with a third variable, except that the gradient has three components and the constraint is now a surface (here, a plane). Picture a sphere growing from the origin until it first touches the plane \(x+y+z=1\).

\(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\)\(\nabla f=\langle 2x,2y,2z\rangle\), \(\nabla g=\langle 1,1,1\rangle\), with \(g=x+y+z-1\)
The equations\(2x_0=2y_0=2z_0=\lambda\), so \(x_0=y_0=z_0\); the constraint gives \(x_0=\tfrac13\)
Minimum value\(f\left(\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13\right)=\mathbf{\tfrac13}\)

Two constraints \(g=0\) and \(h=0\): one multiplier each, and \(\nabla f=\lambda_1\nabla g+\lambda_2\nabla h\) together with both constraints. Geometrically, \(\nabla f\) must lie in the plane spanned by \(\nabla g\) and \(\nabla h\), so \(f\) is not changing along the curve where the two surfaces meet. (Example 4.45 works this out for a cone cut by a plane.)
