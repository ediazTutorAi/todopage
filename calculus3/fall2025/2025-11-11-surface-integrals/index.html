A parameterized surface is given by a description of the form $$\vec r(u,v)=\langle x(u,v),\,y(u,v),\,z(u,v)\rangle.$$ The parameters \(u\) and \(v\) vary over a region in the \(uv\)-plane called the parameter domain.

Definition: surface area

Let \(\vec r(u,v)=\langle x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v)\rangle\) with parameter domain \(D\) be a smooth parameterization of surface \(S\), traced out only once as \((u,v)\) varies over \(D\). The surface area of \(S\) is $$\iint_D\|\vec t_u\times\vec t_v\|\,dA,\qquad \vec t_u=\Big\langle\frac{\partial x}{\partial u},\frac{\partial y}{\partial u},\frac{\partial z}{\partial u}\Big\rangle,\ \vec t_v=\Big\langle\frac{\partial x}{\partial v},\frac{\partial y}{\partial v},\frac{\partial z}{\partial v}\Big\rangle.$$

Curve \(C\): \(\vec r(t)\)Surface \(S\): \(\vec r(u,v)\)
Small piece\(ds=\|\vec r'(t)\|\,dt\)\(dS=\|\vec t_u\times\vec t_v\|\,dA\)
Integral of \(f\)\(\displaystyle\int_Cf\,ds=\int_a^bf(\vec r(t))\|\vec r'(t)\|\,dt\)\(\displaystyle\iint_Sf\,dS=\iint_Df(\vec r(u,v))\|\vec t_u\times\vec t_v\|\,dA\)
Definition: regular (smooth) parameterization

Parameterization \(\vec r(u,v)=\langle x(u,v),y(u,v),z(u,v)\rangle\) is a regular parameterization if \(\vec t_u\times\vec t_v\) is not zero for any point \((u,v)\) in the parameter domain. It is smooth if the vector \(\vec t_u\times\vec t_v\) is not zero for any choice of \(u\) and \(v\) in the parameter domain. (The book writes \(\vec r_u\times\vec r_v\); it is the same vector.)

Example: \(\vec r(u,v)=\langle0,\cos v,1\rangle\) has \(\vec t_u=\vec 0\), so \(\vec t_u\times\vec t_v=\vec 0\) and its image is only a line, not a surface.

On the sphere above, move the rectangle to the north pole (\(u=0\)). What happens to the patch?It shrinks to a point: there \(\vec t_v=\vec 0\), so \(\vec t_u\times\vec t_v=\vec 0\). The sphere parameterization is not regular at the poles.

Which side counts as positive?
Orientation, Example 6.69 and Checkpoint 6.57

Flux counts fluid crossing \(S\) in a chosen direction, so each point of \(S\) needs a chosen unit normal. For any point on a smooth surface \(S\) there are two unit normal vectors \(\vec N\) and \(-\vec N\). If it is possible to choose a unit normal \(\vec N\) at every point so that \(\vec N\) varies continuously over \(S\), then \(S\) is orientable. A closed surface with the outward normal has the positive orientation. For a parameterization, the choice $$\vec N=\frac{\vec t_u\times\vec t_v}{\|\vec t_u\times\vec t_v\|}$$ gives an orientation of \(S\). For \(z=f(x,y)\): \(\vec t_x\times\vec t_y=\langle-f_x,-f_y,1\rangle\), the upward normal.

Cylinder: \(\vec N\) outward. Flip: \(-\vec N\).

Closed surface, outward \(\vec N\): positive orientation.

The Möbius strip (a half-twist, ends glued) has only one side: it is nonorientable, so no vector surface integral is defined over it.

Graph \(z=xy\): upward normal \(\langle-y,-x,1\rangle\).

Möbius strip: going once around reverses \(\vec N\).

What happens to \(\vec N\) if we swap the roles of \(u\) and \(v\)?\(\vec t_v\times\vec t_u=-\vec t_u\times\vec t_v\), so \(\vec N\) becomes \(-\vec N\): the orientation reverses.

An orientation of the cylinder \(x^2+y^2=r^2\), \(0\le z\le h\).\(\vec r(u,v)=\langle r\cos u,r\sin u,v\rangle\), \(\vec t_u\times\vec t_v=\langle r\cos u,r\sin u,0\rangle\), \(\|\cdot\|=r\). \(\vec N(u,v)=\langle\cos u,\sin u,0\rangle\): all vectors point outward, an outward orientation.

How much flows through a surface?
Equations 6.20, 6.21, Example 6.70 and Checkpoint 6.58
Definition: surface integral of a vector field (flux)

Let \(\vec F\) be a continuous vector field with a domain that contains oriented surface \(S\) with unit normal vector \(\vec N\). The surface integral of \(\vec F\) over \(S\) is $$\iint_S\vec F\cdot d\vec S=\iint_S\vec F\cdot\vec N\,dS.$$ It is also called the flux of \(\vec F\) across \(S\). If \(S\) is parameterized by \(\vec r(u,v)\) over \(D\), $$\iint_S\vec F\cdot\vec N\,dS=\iint_D\big(\vec F(\vec r(u,v))\cdot(\vec t_u\times\vec t_v)\big)\,dA.$$

Where did the factor \(\|\vec t_u\times\vec t_v\|\) go? Combine \(\vec N\) and \(dS\).\(\vec N\,dS=\dfrac{\vec t_u\times\vec t_v}{\|\vec t_u\times\vec t_v\|}\,\|\vec t_u\times\vec t_v\|\,dA=(\vec t_u\times\vec t_v)\,dA\). The cross product carries both the area factor and the direction.

Example 6.70: \(\vec F=\langle-y,x,0\rangle\) on \(\vec r(u,v)=\langle u,\ v^2-u,\ u+v\rangle\), \(0\le u<3\), \(0\le v\le4\). Green arrows are the unit normal \(\vec N\), orange arrows are \(\vec F\). Flip the orientation and the flux changes sign.

Example 6.70. Compute \(\iint_S\vec F\cdot\vec N\,dS\). First \(\vec t_u\times\vec t_v\).\(\vec t_u=\langle1,-1,1\rangle\), \(\vec t_v=\langle0,2v,1\rangle\), \(\vec t_u\times\vec t_v=\langle-1-2v,-1,2v\rangle\).
\(\vec F(\vec r(u,v))\cdot(\vec t_u\times\vec t_v)\)\(\vec F(\vec r)=\langle u-v^2,u,0\rangle\), so the integrand is \((u-v^2)(-1-2v)-u\).
The flux\(\displaystyle\int_0^4\!\!\int_0^3\big[(u-v^2)(-1-2v)-u\big]\,du\,dv=\int_0^4(6v^3+3v^2-9v-9)\,dv=340\)

303. \(\iint_S\vec F\cdot\vec N\,dS\), \(\vec F=\langle xy,z,x+y\rangle\), \(S\) the part of \(x+y+z=1\) in the first octant, upward.\(\vec t_x\times\vec t_y=\langle1,1,1\rangle\). \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{1-x}\big[xy+(1-x-y)+(x+y)\big]\,dy\,dx=\dfrac{13}{24}\)
317. \(\iint_S(x\,\mathbf i+y\,\mathbf j)\cdot d\vec S\), \(S\) the cylinder \(x^2+y^2=4\), \(1\le z\le3\), outward.\(\vec F\cdot\vec N=2\) everywhere, area \(2\pi\cdot2\cdot2=8\pi\): \(16\pi\).
Spot the mistake. A student computes \(\iint_Sz\,dS\) over the hemisphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=4\), \(z\ge0\), as \(\iint_D\sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}\,dA\) with \(D:\ x^2+y^2\le4\).The factor \(\|\vec t_x\times\vec t_y\|=\sqrt{1+s_x^2+s_y^2}=2/z\) is missing. The student gets \(16\pi/3\); with it, the integrand is \(z\cdot2/z=2\) and the answer is \(2\cdot4\pi=8\pi\).
Spot the mistake. For the outward flux of \(\vec F=\langle x,y,z\rangle\) through the unit sphere, a student uses \(\vec t_\theta\times\vec t_\varphi\) and gets \(-4\pi\).\(\vec t_\theta\times\vec t_\varphi=-\vec t_\varphi\times\vec t_\theta\) points inward. With \(\vec t_\varphi\times\vec t_\theta\), \(\vec F\cdot\vec N=1\) and the flux is \(4\pi\).
