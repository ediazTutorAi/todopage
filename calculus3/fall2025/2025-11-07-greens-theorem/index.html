Green's Theorem

Extending the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
Idea
Recall: two versions of the Fundamental Theorem

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus says that

$$\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a).$$

The same idea is true of the Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals:

$$\int_C\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf r=f(\mathbf r(b))-f(\mathbf r(a)).$$

When we have a potential function (an "antiderivative"), we can calculate the line integral based solely on information about the boundary of curve \(C\).

Green's theorem

Green's theorem takes this idea and extends it to calculating double integrals. Green's theorem says that we can calculate a double integral over region \(D\) based solely on information about the boundary of \(D\). Green's theorem also says we can calculate a line integral over a simple closed curve \(C\) based solely on information about the region that \(C\) encloses. In particular, Green's theorem connects a double integral over region \(D\) to a line integral around the boundary of \(D\).

In the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, what is the "boundary" of \([a,b]\)?the two endpoints \(a\) and \(b\)
In Green's theorem, what is the boundary of the region \(D\)?the closed curve \(C\) that encloses it
Circulation Form of Green's Theorem
Theorem 6.12
Theorem 6.12: Green's Theorem, Circulation Form

Let \(D\) be an open, simply connected region with a boundary curve \(C\) that is a piecewise smooth, simple closed curve oriented counterclockwise. Let \(\mathbf F=\langle P,Q\rangle\) be a vector field with component functions that have continuous partial derivatives on \(D\). Then,

$$\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\oint_C P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint_D (Q_x-P_y)\,dA.$$
Tangential form

Notice that Green's theorem can be used only for a two-dimensional vector field \(\mathbf F\). If \(\mathbf F\) is a three-dimensional field, then Green's theorem does not apply. Since

$$\int_C P\,dx+Q\,dy=\int_C\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf T\,ds,$$

this version of Green's theorem is sometimes referred to as the tangential form of Green's theorem.

If \(\mathbf F\) is conservative, both sides of the theorem are \(0\). Why?the circulation is zero in a conservative field, and \(P_y=Q_x\) makes \(Q_x-P_y=0\)
Why It Works
Proof for a rectangle
Proof, when \(D\) is a rectangle

Let \(D\) be the rectangle \([a,b]\times[c,d]\) oriented counterclockwise. Then the boundary \(C\) of \(D\) consists of four piecewise smooth pieces \(C_1,C_2,C_3,C_4\). We parameterize each side of \(D\) as follows:

$$C_1:\mathbf r_1(t)=\langle t,c\rangle,\ a\le t\le b\qquad C_2:\mathbf r_2(t)=\langle b,t\rangle,\ c\le t\le d$$ $$-C_3:\mathbf r_3(t)=\langle t,d\rangle,\ a\le t\le b\qquad -C_4:\mathbf r_4(t)=\langle a,t\rangle,\ c\le t\le d.$$
\(\displaystyle\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_{C_1}+\int_{C_2}-\int_{-C_3}-\int_{-C_4}=\)\(\displaystyle\int_a^b\big(P(t,c)-P(t,d)\big)dt+\int_c^d\big(Q(b,t)-Q(a,t)\big)dt\)
By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, \(P(t,d)-P(t,c)=\) and \(Q(b,t)-Q(a,t)=\)\(\displaystyle\int_c^d\frac{\partial}{\partial y}P(t,y)\,dy\) and \(\displaystyle\int_a^b\frac{\partial}{\partial x}Q(x,t)\,dx\)
Why? Fix \(t\) and let \(y\) go from \(c\) to \(d\). A small step \(dy\) changes \(P\) by about\(\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}(t,y)\,dy\) (linear approximation); adding up all these small changes gives the total change \(P(t,d)-P(t,c)\). The same holds for \(Q\) in the \(x\) direction.
Therefore\(\displaystyle\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_a^b\!\!\int_c^d(Q_x-P_y)\,dy\,dx=\iint_D(Q_x-P_y)\,dA\)
General regions

To prove Green's theorem over a general region \(D\), we can decompose \(D\) into many tiny rectangles and use the proof that the theorem works over rectangles.

Applying the Circulation Form
Examples 6.38, 6.39 and Checkpoint 6.34
Example 6.38. \(\displaystyle\int_C x^2y\,dx+(y-3)\,dy\), where \(C\) is a rectangle with vertices \((1,1)\), \((4,1)\), \((4,5)\), \((1,5)\) oriented counterclockwise.\(Q_x-P_y=-x^2\), so \(\displaystyle\int_1^5\!\!\int_1^4-x^2\,dx\,dy=-84\)
Example 6.39. The work done on a particle by \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle y+\sin x,\ e^y-x\rangle\) as it traverses the circle \(x^2+y^2=4\) once counterclockwise.\(Q_x-P_y=-2\), so \(W=-2\,\mathrm{area}(D)=-2\pi(2^2)=-8\pi\)
Checkpoint 6.34. \(\displaystyle\int_C\sin(x^2)\,dx+(3x-y)\,dy\), where \(C\) is a right triangle with vertices \((-1,2)\), \((4,2)\), \((4,5)\) oriented counterclockwise.\(Q_x-P_y=3\), area \(=\tfrac{15}{2}\), so \(\tfrac{45}{2}\)
Area from a Boundary
Example 6.40, Equation 6.14 and Checkpoint 6.35
Example 6.40. The area enclosed by the ellipse \(\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}=1\), \(x=a\cos t\), \(y=b\sin t\), \(0\le t\le 2\pi\). Take \(\mathbf F=\langle-\tfrac y2,\tfrac x2\rangle\). Then \(Q_x-P_y=\)\(\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1\), so \(\displaystyle\iint_D dA=\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi}ab\,dt=\pi ab\)
Equation 6.14: area from the boundary

Let \(D\) be any region with a boundary that is a simple closed curve \(C\) oriented counterclockwise. If \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle-\tfrac y2,\tfrac x2\rangle\), then \(Q_x-P_y=1\). Therefore,

$$\text{area of }D=\iint_D dA=\frac12\oint_C -y\,dx+x\,dy.$$
Checkpoint 6.35. The area of the region enclosed by \(\mathbf r(t)=\langle\sin t\cos t,\ \sin t\rangle\), \(0\le t\le\pi\).\(\tfrac12\int_0^\pi(-y\,x'+x\,y')\,dt=\tfrac12\int_0^\pi\sin^3t\,dt=\tfrac23\)
Flux Form of Green's Theorem
Theorem 6.13
Theorem 6.13: Green's Theorem, Flux Form

Let \(D\) be an open, simply connected region with a boundary curve \(C\) that is a piecewise smooth, simple closed curve that is oriented counterclockwise. Let \(\mathbf F=\langle P,Q\rangle\) be a vector field with component functions that have continuous partial derivatives on an open region containing \(D\). Then,

$$\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf N\,ds=\iint_D (P_x+Q_y)\,dA.$$
Example 6.41. The flux of \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle x,y\rangle\) across a circle of radius \(r\) centered at the origin.\(P_x+Q_y=2\), so \(2\iint_D dA=2\pi r^2\)
Example 6.42. The flux of \(\mathbf F=\langle x^2+e^y,\ x+y\rangle\) across the triangle with vertices \((0,0)\), \((1,0)\), \((0,3)\) oriented clockwise.\(P_x+Q_y=2x+1\); clockwise gives a minus sign: \(-\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{3-3x}(2x+1)\,dy\,dx=-\tfrac52\)
Checkpoint 6.36. The flux of \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle x^3,y^3\rangle\) across a unit circle oriented counterclockwise.\(P_x+Q_y=3(x^2+y^2)\); \(3\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1r^3\,dr\,d\theta=\tfrac{3\pi}2\)
Example 6.43. Water flows with velocity \(\mathbf v=\langle5x+y,\ x+3y\rangle\) m/sec. The amount flowing per second across the rectangle with vertices \((-1,-2)\), \((1,-2)\), \((1,3)\), \((-1,3)\), counterclockwise.\(P_x+Q_y=8\), so \(8\cdot(\text{area }10)=80\) m2/sec
Source-Free Fields
Examples 6.44, 6.45 and Checkpoints
Source-free vector fields

If we replace "circulation of \(\mathbf F\)" with "flux of \(\mathbf F\)," then we get a definition of a source-free vector field. The following statements are all equivalent ways of defining a source-free field \(\mathbf F=\langle P,Q\rangle\) on a simply connected domain:

  1. The flux \(\displaystyle\int_C\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf N\,ds\) across any closed curve \(C\) is zero.
  2. If \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are curves in the domain of \(\mathbf F\) with the same starting points and endpoints, then \(\displaystyle\int_{C_1}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf N\,ds=\int_{C_2}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf N\,ds\). In other words, flux is independent of path.
  3. There is a stream function \(g(x,y)\) for \(\mathbf F\): a function \(g\) such that \(P=g_y\) and \(Q=-g_x\).
  4. \(P_x+Q_y=0\).
Example 6.44. Verify that the rotation field \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle y,-x\rangle\) is source free, and find a stream function.\(P_x+Q_y=0+0=0\). From \(g_y=y\): \(g=\tfrac{y^2}2+h(x)\); \(-g_x=-x\) gives \(h'=x\); \(g=\tfrac{x^2}2+\tfrac{y^2}2\)
Checkpoint 6.37. A stream function for \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle x\sin y,\ \cos y\rangle\).\(P_x+Q_y=\sin y-\sin y=0\); \(g(x,y)=-x\cos y\)
Harmonic functions

Any potential function \(f\) of a conservative and source-free vector field on a simply connected domain satisfies Laplace's equation \(f_{xx}+f_{yy}=0\). A function that satisfies Laplace's equation is called a harmonic function.

Example 6.45. For \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle e^x\sin y,\ e^x\cos y\rangle\): conservative? source free? A potential function? Is it harmonic?\(P_y=e^x\cos y=Q_x\); \(P_x+Q_y=e^x\sin y-e^x\sin y=0\); \(f=e^x\sin y\); \(f_{xx}+f_{yy}=0\)
Checkpoint 6.38. Is \(f(x,y)=e^{x+5y}\) harmonic?no: \(f_{xx}+f_{yy}=e^{x+5y}+25e^{x+5y}\neq0\)
Green's Theorem on General Regions
Regions with holes, Examples 6.46 and 6.47
Orientation of the boundary

Let \(D\) be a region and let \(C\) be a component of the boundary of \(D\). We say that \(C\) is positively oriented if, as we walk along \(C\) in the direction of orientation, region \(D\) is always on our left. Curve \(C\) is negatively oriented if, as we walk along \(C\) in the direction of orientation, region \(D\) is always on our right.

Let \(D\) be a region with finitely many holes and denote the boundary of \(D\) by \(\partial D\), with the inner holes given a negative orientation and the outer boundary a positive orientation. Then

$$\int_{\partial D}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\iint_D (Q_x-P_y)\,dA\qquad\text{and}\qquad \int_{\partial D}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf N\,ds=\iint_D (P_x+Q_y)\,dA.$$
Example 6.46. \(\displaystyle\oint_{\partial D}\Big(\sin x-\frac{y^3}3\Big)dx+\Big(\frac{x^3}3+\sin y\Big)dy\), where \(D\) is the annulus \(1\le r\le2\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\).\(Q_x-P_y=x^2+y^2\); \(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_1^2r^3\,dr\,d\theta=\tfrac{15\pi}2\)
Example 6.47. \(\mathbf F=\Big\langle\dfrac{y}{x^2+y^2},\dfrac{-x}{x^2+y^2}\Big\rangle\) and \(C\) any simple closed curve oriented counterclockwise. What are the possible values of \(\displaystyle\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\)?\(0\) if \(C\) does not encompass the origin; \(-2\pi\) if it does (\(\displaystyle\int_C=-\int_{C_1}\), \(C_1\) a clockwise circle about the origin inside \(C\))
Checkpoint 6.39. \(\displaystyle\int_{\partial D}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\), \(D\) the annulus \(2\le r\le5\), \(\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle x^3,\ 5x+e^y\sin y\rangle\).\(Q_x-P_y=5\), area \(=21\pi\), so \(105\pi\)
