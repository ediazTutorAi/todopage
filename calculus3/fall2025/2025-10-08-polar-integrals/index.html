Double Integrals in Polar Coordinates

When Rectangles Fight Back
A disk is easy to say and hard to slice

Last lesson we integrated over regions \(D\) by slicing with vertical or horizontal lines. For the disk \(x^2+y^2\le4\) that meant inner limits \(\pm\sqrt{4-x^2}\) and a square root in every integrand. But the disk is described by two simple conditions in polar coordinates: \(0\le r\le2\) and \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\).

The same disk, now swept by a ray instead of a vertical slice. Press Sweep. Compare where the ray starts and stops with the slice from last lesson.

What are the limits of \(r\) on every ray? What does that tell you about the iterated integral?

Every ray runs from \(r=0\) to \(r=2\): constant limits, just like a rectangle, but in the \((r,\theta)\) variables. Circles, rings, sectors and petals become "rectangles" in polar coordinates.

Goal for today: rewrite \(\iint_D f(x,y)\,dA\) in \(r\) and \(\theta\). The only tricky part is what replaces \(dA\).

Polar Rectangles and the Factor r
Where does \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\) come from?

A polar rectangle is \(R=\{(r,\theta)\mid a\le r\le b,\ \alpha\le\theta\le\beta\}\): its sides are circle arcs (constant \(r\)) and rays (constant \(\theta\)).

Example 5.24. \(R=\{1\le r\le3,\ 0\le\theta\le\pi\}\). Cut it into \(m\) rings and \(n\) wedges. The red piece is one subrectangle \(R_{ij}\). Drag ring to move it outward and watch its two arcs and its area.

Describe the picture in wordsThe upper half of a ring (annulus) with inner radius 1 and outer radius 3.
Is the red piece's area \(\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\)?No. Move the ring slider: the area grows as the piece moves outward, because the same \(\Delta\theta\) subtends a longer arc at larger \(r\).
Exact area of a piece\(\Delta A=\tfrac12(r_i^2-r_{i-1}^2)\Delta\theta=\tfrac12(r_i+r_{i-1})(r_i-r_{i-1})\Delta\theta=r^*\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\), where \(r^*\) is the midpoint radius.
What do the two sums in the readout say?\(\sum r^*\Delta r\Delta\theta\) matches the true area \(\tfrac12(b^2-a^2)(\beta-\alpha)\). Forgetting the factor \(r\) gives the wrong number, however fine the grid.
Double Integral over a Polar Rectangle
Definition and the iterated form

Sampling \(f\) at \((r_{ij}^*,\theta_{ij}^*)\) in each piece, the Riemann sum and its limit define

$$\iint_R f(r,\theta)\,dA=\lim_{m,n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{j=1}^n f(r_{ij}^*,\theta_{ij}^*)\,r_{ij}^*\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta .$$

In an iterated integral, \(dA\) becomes \(r\,dr\,d\theta\), and \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\):

$$\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA=\int_{\theta=\alpha}^{\beta}\int_{r=a}^{b}f(r\cos\theta,\,r\sin\theta)\;r\,dr\,d\theta .$$

Example 5.25. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_R 3x\,dA\) over \(R=\{1\le r\le2,\ 0\le\theta\le\pi\}\).

Convert the integrand\(3x=3r\cos\theta\), and \(dA=r\,dr\,d\theta\), so the integrand is \(3r^2\cos\theta\).
Set up\(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi}\!\!\int_1^2 3r^2\cos\theta\,dr\,d\theta\)
EvaluateThe limits are constant and the integrand separates: \(\displaystyle\Big(\int_1^2 3r^2\,dr\Big)\Big(\int_0^\pi\cos\theta\,d\theta\Big)=7\cdot0=\mathbf{0}\).
Does 0 make sense?Yes. \(R\) is symmetric about the \(y\)-axis and \(x\) is odd in that symmetry, so the left half cancels the right half.
Example 5.26 and 5.27
Disks and half rings

Example 5.26. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_R(1-x^2-y^2)\,dA\) over the unit disk. Also the volume under the paraboloid \(z=1-x^2-y^2\) (Example 5.29).

Region\(0\le r\le1,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\)
Use \(x^2+y^2=r^2\)\(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1(1-r^2)\,r\,dr\,d\theta\)
Evaluate\(\displaystyle2\pi\int_0^1(r-r^3)\,dr=2\pi\Big(\tfrac12-\tfrac14\Big)=\mathbf{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\)
Rectangular version?\(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1\!\!\int_{-\sqrt{1-x^2}}^{\sqrt{1-x^2}}(1-x^2-y^2)\,dy\,dx\) is possible, and much longer.

Example 5.27. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_R(x+y)\,dA\) where \(R=\{1\le x^2+y^2\le4,\ x\le0\}\). The region, swept by a ray:

Polar description\(1\le r\le2,\ \tfrac{\pi}{2}\le\theta\le\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\) (the left half, since \(x\le0\))
Set up\(\displaystyle\int_{\pi/2}^{3\pi/2}\!\!\int_1^2(r\cos\theta+r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta\)
Evaluate\(\displaystyle\Big(\int_1^2r^2dr\Big)\Big(\int_{\pi/2}^{3\pi/2}(\cos\theta+\sin\theta)\,d\theta\Big)=\tfrac73\cdot(-2)=\mathbf{-\tfrac{14}{3}}\)
General Polar Regions
Theorem 5.8: the inner limits can depend on \(\theta\)

If \(D=\{(r,\theta)\mid\alpha\le\theta\le\beta,\ h_1(\theta)\le r\le h_2(\theta)\}\) and \(f\) is continuous, then

$$\iint_D f(r,\theta)\,dA=\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\int_{h_1(\theta)}^{h_2(\theta)}f(r,\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta .$$

Same idea as Type I and Type II, but the slice is a ray: the outer variable is \(\theta\), and the ray enters at \(h_1(\theta)\) and leaves at \(h_2(\theta)\). Here is the upper half of the cardioid \(r=1+\cos\theta\).

Example 5.28. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_D r^2\sin\theta\;r\,dr\,d\theta\) over this region.

Set up\(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi}\!\!\int_0^{1+\cos\theta}r^3\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\)
Inner integral\(\displaystyle\int_0^{1+\cos\theta}r^3\sin\theta\,dr=\tfrac14(1+\cos\theta)^4\sin\theta\)
Substitute \(u=1+\cos\theta\)\(du=-\sin\theta\,d\theta\), and \(\theta:0\to\pi\) gives \(u:2\to0\). So \(\displaystyle\tfrac14\int_0^2u^4\,du=\tfrac14\cdot\tfrac{32}{5}=\mathbf{\tfrac85}\).
Volumes in Polar Coordinates
Examples 5.30 and 5.32

Example 5.30. Find the volume under \(z=4-x^2-y^2\) and above the disk \((x-1)^2+y^2=1\). The disk is not centered at the origin, but it still has a simple polar boundary.

Boundary in polar form\(x^2+y^2=2x\Rightarrow r^2=2r\cos\theta\Rightarrow r=2\cos\theta\)
Range of \(\theta\)\(r\ge0\) needs \(\cos\theta\ge0\): \(-\tfrac\pi2\le\theta\le\tfrac\pi2\). Check it with the ray above. (Not \(0\le\theta\le\pi\).)
Set up\(\displaystyle V=\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{2\cos\theta}(4-r^2)\,r\,dr\,d\theta\)
Inner integral\(\displaystyle\Big[2r^2-\tfrac{r^4}{4}\Big]_0^{2\cos\theta}=8\cos^2\theta-4\cos^4\theta\)
Outer integralWith \(\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta=\tfrac\pi2\) and \(\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^4\theta=\tfrac{3\pi}{8}\): \(V=4\pi-\tfrac{3\pi}{2}=\mathbf{\tfrac{5\pi}{2}}\).

Example 5.32. The cone \(z=2-\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) above the \(xy\)-plane.

Base and heightThe cone meets the plane where \(r=2\): base is the disk \(0\le r\le2\), and \(z=2-r\).
Volume\(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2(2-r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\Big[r^2-\tfrac{r^3}{3}\Big]_0^2=\mathbf{\tfrac{8\pi}{3}}\), which is \(\tfrac13\pi a^2h\) for \(a=h=2\).
Areas in Polar Coordinates
Take \(f=1\): then \(A(D)=\iint_D r\,dr\,d\theta\)

Example 5.34. Find the area of the region inside both the circle \(r=3\cos\theta\) and the cardioid \(r=1+\cos\theta\). The dashed curve is the circle. The boundary switches from the cardioid to the circle where the curves cross.

Where do the curves cross?\(3\cos\theta=1+\cos\theta\Rightarrow\cos\theta=\tfrac12\Rightarrow\theta=\pm\tfrac\pi3\)
Which curve is the outer limit?On \(0\le\theta\le\tfrac\pi3\) the cardioid is smaller, so \(h_2=1+\cos\theta\). On \(\tfrac\pi3\le\theta\le\tfrac\pi2\) the circle is, so \(h_2=3\cos\theta\). Use symmetry about the \(x\)-axis.
Set up\(\displaystyle A=2\Big[\int_0^{\pi/3}\!\!\int_0^{1+\cos\theta}r\,dr\,d\theta+\int_{\pi/3}^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{3\cos\theta}r\,dr\,d\theta\Big]\)
Evaluate\(\displaystyle\tfrac12\int_0^{\pi/3}(1+\cos\theta)^2d\theta=\tfrac\pi4+\tfrac{9\sqrt3}{16}\) and \(\displaystyle\tfrac92\int_{\pi/3}^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta=\tfrac{3\pi}{8}-\tfrac{9\sqrt3}{16}\). The \(\sqrt3\) terms cancel, and doubling gives \(A=\mathbf{\tfrac{5\pi}{4}}\).

Example 5.33 (symmetry). The eight-petal rose \(r=\cos4\theta\) has area \(8\int_{-\pi/8}^{\pi/8}\!\int_0^{\cos4\theta}r\,dr\,d\theta=\tfrac\pi2\): one petal is \(-\tfrac\pi8\le\theta\le\tfrac\pi8\).

An Improper Integral
Example 5.35: why polar coordinates matter beyond disks

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb R^2}e^{-10(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy\). In rectangular coordinates \(\int e^{-x^2}dx\) has no elementary antiderivative. In polar coordinates the extra factor \(r\) makes it work.

Integrate over the disk of radius \(a\) (the simpler case \(e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\)): the value \(\pi(1-e^{-a^2})\) against \(a\). Dashed: \(\pi\).

Integrate over the disk \(r\le a\)\(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^ae^{-10r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\cdot\tfrac{1-e^{-10a^2}}{20}=\tfrac{\pi}{10}\big(1-e^{-10a^2}\big)\)
Let \(a\to\infty\)\(e^{-10a^2}\to0\), so the integral over the whole plane is \(\mathbf{\tfrac{\pi}{10}}\).
Why the factor \(r\) helps\(r\,e^{-10r^2}\) has the antiderivative \(-\tfrac1{20}e^{-10r^2}\). Without the \(r\) there is none.

Summary
The one-page version
Conversion\(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta,\ x^2+y^2=r^2,\ \boxed{dA=r\,dr\,d\theta}\). Never forget the \(r\).
Polar rectangle\(\displaystyle\iint_R f\,dA=\int_\alpha^\beta\!\!\int_a^b f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta\)
General region\(\displaystyle\int_\alpha^\beta\!\!\int_{h_1(\theta)}^{h_2(\theta)}f\,r\,dr\,d\theta\). Sweep a ray: it enters at \(h_1\), leaves at \(h_2\).
When to use itThe region is a disk, ring, sector or petal, or the integrand contains \(x^2+y^2\).
UsesVolume under \(z=f\), area \(\iint_D r\,dr\,d\theta\), improper integrals over \(\mathbb R^2\).
