Cylindrical and Spherical Integrals

Cylindrical Coordinates
Polar coordinates in the plane, plus the height z

When a solid has a circular axis of symmetry, the polar \((r,\theta)\) we already use in the \(xy\)-plane is the natural start. Keep it, and keep \(z\) as it is: a point is \((r,\theta,z)\).

$$x=r\cos\theta,\quad y=r\sin\theta,\quad z=z \qquad\qquad r^2=x^2+y^2,\quad \tan\theta=\frac yx,\quad z=z$$

Move the three sliders. The blue segment is \(r\) (along the floor), the orange arc is \(\theta\) (measured from the positive \(x\)-axis), the green segment is the height \(z\). Drag the picture to turn it.

Convert \((r,\theta,z)=(2,\tfrac{2\pi}{3},1)\) to rectangular\(x=2\cos\tfrac{2\pi}3=-1,\ \ y=2\sin\tfrac{2\pi}3=\sqrt3,\ \ z=1\). The readout above shows the same numbers.
Convert \((1,1,\sqrt2)\) to cylindrical\(r=\sqrt{1+1}=\sqrt2,\ \ \theta=\tfrac\pi4,\ \ z=\sqrt2\). So \((\sqrt2,\tfrac\pi4,\sqrt2)\).
Convert \((-1,1,0)\). Why is \(\theta=\tan^{-1}(y/x)\) dangerous?\(\tan^{-1}(-1)=-\tfrac\pi4\) points into quadrant IV, but the point is in quadrant II. Use \(\theta=\tfrac{3\pi}4\). Always check the quadrant of \((x,y)\).
Surfaces in Cylindrical Coordinates
Hold one coordinate constant

In rectangular coordinates, fixing one variable gives a plane (\(x=c\), \(y=c\), \(z=c\)). Fix one cylindrical coordinate instead. Guess before you press: what surface does each give?

Press fix r, fix θ or fix z to draw the surface through the point (the point always lies on it). Press again to clear.

\(r=c\)A cylinder of radius \(c\) around the \(z\)-axis.
\(\theta=c\)A vertical half-plane hinged on the \(z\)-axis (the full plane if \(r\) may be negative).
\(z=c\)A horizontal plane, exactly as in rectangular coordinates.
What are \(z=r\) and \(z=r^2\)?\(z=r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) is a cone; \(z=r^2=x^2+y^2\) is a paraboloid. Any equation with \(x^2+y^2\) in it is a candidate for \(r\).
Spherical Coordinates
Distance from the origin and two angles

When the symmetry is about a point, describe a point by its distance \(\rho\ge0\) from the origin, the same angle \(\theta\) as before, and the angle \(\varphi\in[0,\pi]\) down from the positive \(z\)-axis.

$$x=\rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\quad y=\rho\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\quad z=\rho\cos\varphi \qquad \rho^2=x^2+y^2+z^2$$

The dashed segment on the floor has length \(r=\rho\sin\varphi\): that is the link back to cylindrical coordinates. (Some books swap the names \(\theta\) and \(\varphi\). Here \(\theta\) is always the angle around the \(z\)-axis.)

Convert \((\rho,\theta,\varphi)=(2,\tfrac\pi3,\tfrac\pi6)\) to rectangular\(x=2\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac12,\ \ y=2\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}2=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2,\ \ z=2\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}2=\sqrt3\).
Convert \((1,1,\sqrt2)\) to spherical\(\rho=\sqrt{1+1+2}=2\), \(\theta=\tfrac\pi4\), and \(\cos\varphi=\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\) so \(\varphi=\tfrac\pi4\). Spherical \((2,\tfrac\pi4,\tfrac\pi4)\).
The point \((0,0,-3)\): what are \(\rho,\varphi\), and what is \(\theta\)?\(\rho=3,\ \varphi=\pi\), and \(\theta\) can be anything: on the \(z\)-axis the angle around the axis means nothing. Same happens to \(\theta\) in cylindrical coordinates there.
Surfaces in Spherical Coordinates
The same experiment

Same game. Guess the surface, then press the button. Rotate the picture: the cone is easier to see from the side.

\(\rho=c\)A sphere of radius \(c\) centered at the origin.
\(\theta=c\)A vertical half-plane, the same as in cylindrical coordinates.
\(\varphi=c\)A cone around the \(z\)-axis (\(\varphi=\tfrac\pi2\) is the flat \(xy\)-plane, \(\varphi=\pi\) the negative \(z\)-axis).
Write \(x^2+y^2+z^2=9\) in spherical\(\rho^2=9\), so \(\rho=3\).
Write \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) in spherical\(\rho\cos\varphi=\rho\sin\varphi\), so \(\tan\varphi=1\) and \(\varphi=\tfrac\pi4\). That is the cone through the point on screen.
The Volume Element in Cylindrical Coordinates
Why \(dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz\)

Cut space with the surfaces we just drew: \(r\), \(r+\Delta r\); \(\theta\), \(\theta+\Delta\theta\); \(z\), \(z+\Delta z\). We get a small curved box. Its volume \(\Delta V\) is what the triple Riemann sum multiplies \(f\) by.

Edges: radial \(\Delta r\) (blue), the arc along a circle of radius \(r\) (orange), vertical \(\Delta z\) (green). Slide the position r outward, then change \(d\theta\) and watch the orange edge.

How long is the orange edge?An arc of angle \(\Delta\theta\) on a circle of radius \(r\): \(r\,\Delta\theta\), not \(\Delta\theta\). Move \(r\) outward: same angle, longer edge.
Base area of the pieceAnnular sector: \(\tfrac12[(r+\Delta r)^2-r^2]\Delta\theta=r^*\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\) with \(r^*=r+\tfrac{\Delta r}2\), the middle radius. This is the polar \(dA=r\,dr\,d\theta\) from last week.
Volume of the piece\(\Delta V=r^*\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta\,\Delta z\). The readout agrees exactly. The red naive chip (no \(r\)) is badly off, and worse the farther out you slide.
$$\iiint_E f\,dV=\iiint_E f(r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta,\ z)\;\boxed{r}\;dz\,dr\,d\theta$$
The Volume Element in Spherical Coordinates
Why \(dV=\rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta\)

Same cut with \(\rho,\ \theta,\ \varphi\). Three edges leave the corner, and each has a different length.

The blue edge (change in \(\rho\))Straight along a ray: length \(\Delta\rho\).
The green edge (change in \(\varphi\))An arc of angle \(\Delta\varphi\) on a sphere of radius \(\rho\): length \(\rho\,\Delta\varphi\).
The orange edge (change in \(\theta\))An arc of angle \(\Delta\theta\) around the \(z\)-axis, on a circle of radius \(r=\rho\sin\varphi\) (the dashed segment from the last slides): length \(\rho\sin\varphi\,\Delta\theta\). Slide \(\varphi\) toward \(0\) and it shrinks to nothing at the pole.
Multiply\(\Delta V\approx(\Delta\rho)(\rho\,\Delta\varphi)(\rho\sin\varphi\,\Delta\theta)=\rho^2\sin\varphi\,\Delta\rho\,\Delta\varphi\,\Delta\theta\). The readout compares it to the exact piece; shrink the sizes and the match gets better.
$$\iiint_E f\,dV=\iiint_E f(\rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ \rho\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\ \rho\cos\varphi)\;\boxed{\rho^2\sin\varphi}\;d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta$$
Example: A Cone Under a Paraboloid
Setting up in cylindrical coordinates

Let \(E\) be the solid bounded below by the cone \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) and above by the paraboloid \(z=2-x^2-y^2\). Set up and evaluate \(\displaystyle\iiint_E(x^2+y^2)\,dV\). The yellow disk is the shadow \(D\); the pillar goes from the cone up to the paraboloid.

Rewrite both surfaces in \(r\)Cone: \(z=r\). Paraboloid: \(z=2-r^2\). So \(r\le z\le2-r^2\).
Where do they meet? This gives \(D\).\(r=2-r^2\Rightarrow r^2+r-2=0\Rightarrow r=1\). So \(D\) is the disk \(0\le r\le1\), \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\).
The integrand \(x^2+y^2\)\(r^2\). Together with the Jacobian \(r\): the integrand becomes \(r^3\).
Set up\(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_r^{2-r^2}r^2\cdot r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\)
EvaluateInner: \(r^3(2-r^2-r)\). Then \(\int_0^1(2r^3-r^5-r^4)\,dr=\tfrac12-\tfrac16-\tfrac15=\tfrac2{15}\). Times \(2\pi\): \(\ \mathbf{\tfrac{4\pi}{15}}\).
Volume of the same solid?Use \(f=1\): \(\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1(2-r^2-r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\big(1-\tfrac14-\tfrac13\big)=\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\).
Example: The Ice Cream Cone
Setting up in spherical coordinates

Let \(E\) be the solid above the cone \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) and inside the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=4\). Find its volume and \(\displaystyle\iiint_E z\,dV\). Sweep the plane \(z=t\): the readout gives the volume and the integral of \(z\).

Both surfaces in sphericalSphere: \(\rho=2\). Cone: \(\varphi=\tfrac\pi4\) (from the surfaces step). The solid is the part with smaller \(\varphi\), so \(0\le\varphi\le\tfrac\pi4\).
All three ranges\(0\le\rho\le2,\quad 0\le\varphi\le\tfrac\pi4,\quad 0\le\theta\le2\pi\). Constants! That is the whole point of choosing spherical here.
Volume: set up and evaluate\(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/4}\!\!\int_0^2\rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta=\tfrac83\cdot\Big(1-\tfrac{\sqrt2}2\Big)\cdot2\pi=\tfrac{8\pi}3(2-\sqrt2)\approx4.91\)
\(\iiint_E z\,dV\): the integrand\(z=\rho\cos\varphi\), so the integrand is \(\rho\cos\varphi\cdot\rho^2\sin\varphi=\rho^3\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\).
Evaluate\(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/4}\!\!\int_0^2\rho^3\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta=2\pi\cdot4\cdot\tfrac14=\mathbf{2\pi}\approx6.28\)
Compare with the readoutV(E) = 4.907 and \(\iiint_E z\,dV=6.283\). The average value of \(z\) is \(\tfrac{2\pi}{V}\approx1.28\).
Converting an Iterated Integral
Reading the region off the limits

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_{-2}^{2}\int_{-\sqrt{4-x^2}}^{\sqrt{4-x^2}}\int_{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}^{2}(x^2+y^2)\,dz\,dy\,dx\). Read the limits first and picture \(E\); the picture below is that solid. Sweep \(z\) upward.

What is the shadow \(D\)?\(-\sqrt{4-x^2}\le y\le\sqrt{4-x^2}\), \(-2\le x\le2\) is the disk \(x^2+y^2\le4\): \(0\le r\le2,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\).
What is \(E\)?Between the cone \(z=r\) and the plane \(z=2\): an upside-down cone (a party hat balanced on its tip).
Rewrite in cylindrical\(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2\!\!\int_r^2r^2\cdot r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\)
Evaluate\(\int_r^2r^3\,dz=r^3(2-r)\), \(\int_0^2(2r^3-r^4)\,dr=8-\tfrac{32}5=\tfrac85\). Times \(2\pi\): \(\ \mathbf{\tfrac{16\pi}5}\approx10.05\). The sweep readout ends at the same number.
Why was Cartesian a bad choice?The first integral is easy, but the \(x\) and \(y\) integrals of \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\)-type expressions over a disk are miserable. In polar form the disk has constant limits and the \(r\)'s multiply out.
Choosing the Coordinates
And an integral that only spherical can do
\(x^2+y^2\) appears; axis of symmetry is the \(z\)-axis; bounded by cylinders, planes, paraboloidsCylindrical.
\(x^2+y^2+z^2\) appears; spheres, balls, cones with the vertex at the originSpherical. The integrand \(\rho\) and boundary \(\rho=c\) both become trivial.
A cone \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) with a flat top \(z=c\)Either, but cylindrical is shorter (\(r\le z\le c\)). With a spherical cap on top, spherical is shorter.

Example. Let \(B\) be the unit ball \(x^2+y^2+z^2\le1\). Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iiint_B e^{(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\,dV\). There is no elementary antiderivative in \(x,y,z\), so rectangular coordinates are hopeless. Sweep to watch the slices add up.

The integrand and the ball\((x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}=\rho^3\). Ball: \(0\le\rho\le1,\ 0\le\varphi\le\pi,\ 0\le\theta\le2\pi\).
Set up\(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi}\!\!\int_0^1e^{\rho^3}\,\rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta\)
Evaluate. What substitution is waiting?The Jacobian supplies \(\rho^2\), exactly the derivative of \(\rho^3\) up to a factor: \(\int_0^1\rho^2e^{\rho^3}d\rho=\tfrac13(e-1)\). Then \(\int_0^\pi\sin\varphi\,d\varphi=2\) and \(\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta=2\pi\): \(\ \mathbf{\tfrac{4\pi}3(e-1)}\approx7.20\).
Check the machineryWith \(f=1\) and radius \(R\): \(\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\!\!\int_0^R\rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta=\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3\). The volume of a ball, with no tricks.
Summary
The one-page version
Cylindrical\(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta,\ z=z\). \(\ dV=\boxed{r}\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\). Surfaces: \(r=c\) cylinder, \(\theta=c\) half-plane, \(z=c\) plane.
Spherical\(x=\rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ y=\rho\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\ z=\rho\cos\varphi\). \(\ dV=\boxed{\rho^2\sin\varphi}\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta\). Surfaces: \(\rho=c\) sphere, \(\theta=c\) half-plane, \(\varphi=c\) cone.
Why the extra factorCoordinate pieces are curved: arc lengths are \(r\,d\theta\), \(\rho\,d\varphi\), \(\rho\sin\varphi\,d\theta\), not the bare angle.
ProcedureSketch \(E\). Rewrite the boundary surfaces. Find the shadow or the angle ranges. Convert \(f\). Multiply by the Jacobian. Integrate inside out.
Link\(r=\rho\sin\varphi\) and \(z=\rho\cos\varphi\) turn cylindrical into spherical.
Answers today\(\tfrac{4\pi}{15}\), \(\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\), \(\tfrac{8\pi}{3}(2-\sqrt2)\), \(2\pi\), \(\tfrac{16\pi}{5}\), \(\tfrac{4\pi}{3}(e-1)\).
