3.2 Derivatives of Vector-Valued Functions
Definition & Theorem 3.2
Just like scalar functions, the derivative of a vector-valued function $\mathbf{r}(t)$ is defined as a limit: $$\mathbf{r}'(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\mathbf{r}(t+\Delta t) - \mathbf{r}(t)}{\Delta t}$$ provided the limit exists. In practice we never compute this limit directly. Theorem 3.2 says a vector-valued function is differentiated component by component: $$\mathbf{r}(t) = f(t)\mathbf{i}+g(t)\mathbf{j}+h(t)\mathbf{k} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{r}'(t) = f'(t)\mathbf{i}+g'(t)\mathbf{j}+h'(t)\mathbf{k}$$ provided $f$, $g$, $h$ are all differentiable at $t$.
Example
Computing Derivatives
(a) $\mathbf{r}(t) = (6t+8)\mathbf{i}+(4t^2+2t-3)\mathbf{j}$
$\mathbf{r}'(t) = $ \(6\mathbf{i}+(8t+2)\mathbf{j}\)

(b) $\mathbf{r}(t) = 3\cos t\,\mathbf{i}+4\sin t\,\mathbf{j}$
$\mathbf{r}'(t) = $ \(-3\sin t\,\mathbf{i}+4\cos t\,\mathbf{j}\)

(c) $\mathbf{r}(t) = e^t\sin t\,\mathbf{i}+e^t\cos t\,\mathbf{j}-e^{2t}\mathbf{k}$
$\mathbf{r}'(t) = $ \(e^t(\sin t+\cos t)\mathbf{i}+e^t(\cos t-\sin t)\mathbf{j}-2e^{2t}\mathbf{k}\)

(c) needs the product rule on each component. The vector rule is just Theorem 3.2 applied three times.

Tangent Vectors
Definition & the Unit Tangent Vector
Geometrically, $\mathbf{r}'(t_0)$ (when it's nonzero) is a vector tangent to the curve $C$ at the point $\mathbf{r}(t_0)$, pointing in the direction of increasing $t$. A curve is called smooth on an interval if $\mathbf{r}'(t)$ is continuous and never $\mathbf{0}$ there (no corners, cusps, or momentary stops).

Normalizing the tangent vector gives the principal unit tangent vector: $$\mathbf{T}(t) = \frac{\mathbf{r}'(t)}{\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|}, \qquad \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| \neq 0$$ $\mathbf{T}(t)$ always has length $1$ and points in the direction of travel. It will reappear constantly for the rest of this lecture.
Example
Finding the Unit Tangent Vector
(a) $\mathbf{r}(t) = \cos t\,\mathbf{i}+\sin t\,\mathbf{j}$ (the unit circle)
$\mathbf{r}'(t) = -\sin t\,\mathbf{i}+\cos t\,\mathbf{j}$, and $\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| = $ \(\sqrt{\sin^2t+\cos^2t}=1\), so $\mathbf{T}(t) = $ \(-\sin t\,\mathbf{i}+\cos t\,\mathbf{j}\)

Here $\mathbf{T}(t)=\mathbf{r}'(t)$ already: no rescaling needed, since the unit circle happens to already be traced at unit speed.

(b) $\mathbf{u}(t) = (3t^2+2t)\mathbf{i}+(2-4t^3)\mathbf{j}+(6t+5)\mathbf{k}$ at a general $t$: \(\dfrac{(6t+2)\mathbf{i}-12t^2\mathbf{j}+6\mathbf{k}}{\sqrt{36t^4+9t^2+6t+10}}\)
Theorem 3.3: Properties of the Derivative
Rules for differentiating combinations of vector-valued functions
For differentiable $\mathbf{r}(t)$, $\mathbf{u}(t)$, and differentiable scalar function $f(t)$:
  • Scalar multiple: $\dfrac{d}{dt}\big[c\,\mathbf{r}(t)\big] = c\,\mathbf{r}'(t)$
  • Sum/difference: $\dfrac{d}{dt}\big[\mathbf{r}(t)\pm\mathbf{u}(t)\big] = \mathbf{r}'(t)\pm\mathbf{u}'(t)$
  • Scalar-function product: $\dfrac{d}{dt}\big[f(t)\mathbf{u}(t)\big] = f'(t)\mathbf{u}(t)+f(t)\mathbf{u}'(t)$
  • Dot product: $\dfrac{d}{dt}\big[\mathbf{r}(t)\cdot\mathbf{u}(t)\big] = \mathbf{r}'(t)\cdot\mathbf{u}(t)+\mathbf{r}(t)\cdot\mathbf{u}'(t)$
  • Cross product: $\dfrac{d}{dt}\big[\mathbf{r}(t)\times\mathbf{u}(t)\big] = \mathbf{r}'(t)\times\mathbf{u}(t)+\mathbf{r}(t)\times\mathbf{u}'(t)$
  • Chain rule: $\dfrac{d}{dt}\big[\mathbf{r}(f(t))\big] = \mathbf{r}'(f(t))\cdot f'(t)$

All exactly mirror the scalar product/quotient/chain rules. Only the dot and cross product rules are genuinely new, and even those keep the order of $\mathbf{r}$ and $\mathbf{u}$ fixed (crucial for cross products, which don't commute).

A Useful Corollary
If $\|\mathbf{r}(t)\|$ is constant, then $$\mathbf{r}(t)\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t) = 0$$
Example
Applying the Properties
Let $\mathbf{r}(t) = (6t+8)\mathbf{i}+(4t^2+2t-3)\mathbf{j}+5t\,\mathbf{k}$ and $\mathbf{u}(t) = (t^2-3)\mathbf{i}+(2t+4)\mathbf{j}+(t^3-3t)\mathbf{k}$.

$\dfrac{d}{dt}\big[\mathbf{r}(t)\cdot\mathbf{u}(t)\big] = $ \(20t^3+42t^2+26t-16\)

$\dfrac{d}{dt}\big[\mathbf{u}(t)\times\mathbf{u}'(t)\big] = $ \((12t^2+24t)\mathbf{i}+(12t-4t^3)\mathbf{j}-(4t+8)\mathbf{k}\)

Both follow immediately from Theorem 3.3. No need to expand the dot/cross product first and differentiate term by term, though that would also work.

Integrals of Vector-Valued Functions
Definition & Example
Integration is likewise componentwise: $$\int \big[f(t)\mathbf{i}+g(t)\mathbf{j}+h(t)\mathbf{k}\big]\,dt = \Big[\int f(t)\,dt\Big]\mathbf{i}+\Big[\int g(t)\,dt\Big]\mathbf{j}+\Big[\int h(t)\,dt\Big]\mathbf{k}$$ (add a constant vector $\mathbf{C}$ for an indefinite integral), and definite integrals work the same way with limits of integration on each component.

$\displaystyle\int\Big[(3t^2+2t)\mathbf{i}+(3t-6)\mathbf{j}+(6t^3+5t^2-4)\mathbf{k}\Big]dt = $ \(\left(t^3+t^2\right)\mathbf{i}+\left(\tfrac{3t^2}{2}-6t\right)\mathbf{j}+ \left(\tfrac{3t^4}{2}+\tfrac{5t^3}{3}-4t\right)\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{C}\)

$\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/3}\Big[\sin 2t\,\mathbf{i}+\tan t\,\mathbf{j}+e^{-2t}\mathbf{k}\Big]dt = $ \(\tfrac34\mathbf{i}+(\ln 2)\mathbf{j}+\left(\tfrac12-\tfrac12e^{-2\pi/3}\right)\mathbf{k}\)
3.3 Arc Length
Theorem 3.4 & Example
The arc length of a curve $C$ defined by $\mathbf{r}(t)= f(t)\mathbf{i}+g(t)\mathbf{j}+h(t)\mathbf{k}$ for $a \leq t \leq b$ is $$L=\int_a^b \sqrt{f'(t)^2+g'(t)^2+h'(t)^2}\,dt=\int_a^b \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|\,dt$$ This is exactly the "speed integrated over time" idea from single-variable calculus, now in 3D. Example: $\mathbf{r}(t) = (3t-2)\mathbf{i}+(4t+5)\mathbf{j}$, $1\le t\le 5$. Here $\mathbf{r}'(t)=3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}$, a constant velocity of magnitude \(\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|=5\), so $$L=\int_1^5 5\,dt$$ $L = $ \(20\)

A straight segment traveled at constant speed $5$ for $4$ units of time, so of course the length is $20$. The formula is only interesting once the integrand isn't constant.

Example
Arc Length of a Space Curve
$\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t\cos t,\ t\sin t,\ 2t\rangle$, $0\le t\le 2\pi$ (a "conical spiral": it winds around the $z$-axis while its horizontal radius $t$ grows).

$\mathbf{r}'(t) = \langle \cos t - t\sin t,\ \sin t+t\cos t,\ 2\rangle$, and expanding $\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|^2$ the cross terms cancel (Pythagorean identity), leaving $$\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| = \sqrt{t^2+5}$$ $$L = \int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{t^2+5}\,dt$$ $L \approx$ \(25.343\)

This integral needs a trig-substitution antiderivative (or a numeric estimate). Most arc-length integrals in 3D aren't solvable in closed elementary form by hand, which is exactly why the setup is the skill being tested, not always the antiderivative.

Arc-Length Parameterization
Theorem 3.5 & Example
The arc-length function measures distance traveled along $C$ starting at $t=a$: $$s(t) = \int_a^t \|\mathbf{r}'(u)\|\,du, \qquad \frac{ds}{dt}=\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|>0$$ A curve is parameterized by arc length when $\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|=1$ for all $t$. Then $t$ literally is distance traveled from the start. Example: $\mathbf{r}(t) = 4\cos t\,\mathbf{i}+4\sin t\,\mathbf{j}$, $t\ge0$ (circle, radius $4$). Since $\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|=4$ is constant, $s(t)=4t$, so $t=s/4$. Substituting back: $\mathbf{r}(s) = $ \(4\cos(s/4)\,\mathbf{i}+4\sin(s/4)\,\mathbf{j}\), for $s\ge0$

Same circle, same shape, just re-labeled so that plugging in $s=6$, say, gives the point exactly $6$ units of arc away from the start, instead of "wherever you are after $6$ seconds."

Curvature
Definition
Let $C$ be a smooth curve with position vector $\mathbf{r}(s)$, parameterized by arc length $s$. The curvature of $C$ is the rate the unit tangent vector turns per unit of distance traveled: $$\kappa = \left\|\frac{d\mathbf{T}}{ds}\right\| = \|\mathbf{T}'(s)\|$$ Intuitively: how sharply the curve bends. A straight line has $\kappa=0$ everywhere ($\mathbf{T}$ never turns); a circle of radius $r$ has the constant curvature $\kappa = 1/r$: a tighter circle (small $r$) bends more sharply (large $\kappa$).

Almost nothing is naturally parameterized by arc length, so the definition above is rarely used directly for computation. That's what the next slide fixes.

Theorem 3.6: Alternative Formulas for Curvature
Computing $\kappa$ from an ordinary parameter $t$
If $C$ is a smooth curve given by $\mathbf{r}(t)$ (any parameter, not necessarily arc length): $$\kappa(t) = \frac{\|\mathbf{T}'(t)\|}{\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|}$$ If $C$ is a curve in space, an equivalent formula avoids finding $\mathbf{T}(t)$ at all: $$\kappa(t) = \frac{\|\mathbf{r}'(t) \times \mathbf{r}''(t)\|}{\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|^3}$$ If $C$ is a plane curve given explicitly by $y=f(x)$: $$\kappa(x) = \frac{|f''(x)|}{\big(1+[f'(x)]^2\big)^{3/2}}$$

Same quantity, three formulas: pick whichever fits how the curve is given to you. The cross-product version is usually fastest for a 3D $\mathbf{r}(t)$.

Example
Curvature of a Helix
$\mathbf{r}(t) = 4\cos t\,\mathbf{i}+4\sin t\,\mathbf{j}+3t\,\mathbf{k}$.

$\mathbf{r}'(t) = -4\sin t\,\mathbf{i}+4\cos t\,\mathbf{j}+3\mathbf{k}$, so $\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|=5$ (constant, since this helix is traced at constant speed).
$\mathbf{r}''(t) = -4\cos t\,\mathbf{i}-4\sin t\,\mathbf{j}$, and $\mathbf{r}'(t)\times\mathbf{r}''(t) = 12\sin t\,\mathbf{i}-12\cos t\,\mathbf{j}+16\mathbf{k}$, giving $\|\mathbf{r}'\times\mathbf{r}''\| = 20$. Therefore $$\kappa(t) = \frac{20}{5^3}$$ so $\kappa(t) = $ \(\dfrac{4}{25}\)

Notice $t$ dropped out: this helix bends by exactly the same amount at every point, just like a circle does, since it's a circle "stretched" upward at a constant rate.

Example
Curvature of a Plane Curve
$y=f(x)=x^2$. Then $f'(x)=2x$ and $f''(x)=2$, so $$\kappa(x) = \frac{|2|}{(1+4x^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{2}{(1+4x^2)^{3/2}}$$ At the vertex, $\kappa(0) = $ \(2\), the sharpest bend on the whole parabola. As $|x|\to\infty$, $\kappa(x)\to$ \(0\): far from the vertex, the parabola flattens out and looks almost like a straight line.
The Normal and Binormal Vectors
Definitions & the Osculating Circle
If $\mathbf{T}'(t)\neq\mathbf{0}$, the principal unit normal vector is $$\mathbf{N}(t) = \frac{\mathbf{T}'(t)}{\|\mathbf{T}'(t)\|}$$ It points in the direction the curve is turning, and (since $\|\mathbf{T}(t)\|=1$ is constant) the corollary from Step 6 guarantees $\mathbf{N}(t)\perp\mathbf{T}(t)$ automatically. The binormal vector $$\mathbf{B}(t) = \mathbf{T}(t)\times\mathbf{N}(t)$$ is perpendicular to both, completing a moving 3D frame $\{\mathbf{T},\mathbf{N},\mathbf{B}\}$ that travels along the curve (the TNB frame, or Frenet frame).

The osculating circle at a point $P$ is the circle in the plane of $\mathbf{T}$ and $\mathbf{N}$, on the concave side of the curve, tangent to it at $P$, with radius $1/\kappa$: the circle that best approximates the curve right at $P$.
Example
Finding N(t) and B(t)
$\mathbf{r}(t) = (6t+2)\mathbf{i}+5t^2\mathbf{j}-8t\,\mathbf{k}$.

$\mathbf{r}'(t)=6\mathbf{i}+10t\,\mathbf{j}-8\mathbf{k}$, $\|\mathbf{r}'(t)\|=10\sqrt{t^2+1}$, so $\mathbf{T}(t) = $ \(\dfrac{3\mathbf{i}+5t\,\mathbf{j}-4\mathbf{k}}{5\sqrt{t^2+1}}\)

Differentiating $\mathbf{T}(t)$ and normalizing (the algebra is routine but tedious: quotient rule, then simplify):
$\mathbf{N}(t) = $ \(\dfrac{-3t\,\mathbf{i}-5\mathbf{j}-4t\,\mathbf{k}}{5\sqrt{t^2+1}}\)
$\mathbf{B}(t) = \mathbf{T}(t)\times\mathbf{N}(t) = $ \(\dfrac45\mathbf{i}+\dfrac35\mathbf{k}\)

$\mathbf{B}(t)$ came out constant: this curve always bends within parallel planes, all sharing the same normal direction $\mathbf{B}$.

Example
The Osculating Circle
$y=x^3-3x+1$ at $x=1$. Here $y'=3x^2-3$ and $y''=6x$, so at $x=1$: $y'(1)=0$ and $y''(1)=6$, giving $$\kappa(1) = \frac{|6|}{(1+0)^{3/2}}$$ so $\kappa(1) = $ \(6\), giving radius $R = \dfrac1\kappa = $ \(\dfrac16\).

The point on the curve is $(1, f(1)) = (1,-1)$. Since $y'(1)=0$, the curve is momentarily horizontal there, so the osculating circle's center sits directly above (the curve is concave up at $x=1$, since $y''(1)>0$), a distance $R$ away: center $=$ \(\left(1,\ -\dfrac56\right)\), giving $(x-1)^2+\left(y+\dfrac56\right)^2 = $ \(\dfrac{1}{36}\)
3.4 Motion in Space
Velocity, Acceleration, and Speed
If $\mathbf{r}(t)$ is the position of an object at time $t$, then exactly as in one-variable motion:
  • Velocity: $\mathbf{v}(t) = \mathbf{r}'(t)$
  • Acceleration: $\mathbf{a}(t) = \mathbf{v}'(t) = \mathbf{r}''(t)$
  • Speed (a scalar, not a vector): $\|\mathbf{v}(t)\| = \|\mathbf{r}'(t)\| = \dfrac{ds}{dt}$
Nothing new here mathematically. This is just Section 3.2's derivative machinery, applied to a position vector and given physical names.
