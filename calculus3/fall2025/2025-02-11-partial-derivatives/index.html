What this idea reminds you of?
What could be a practical way to compute this rate of change?
How do you define "direction" in the domain of $f$?

Definition
Partial Derivatives
Let $f(x,y)$ be a function of two variables. Then the partial derivative of f with respect to x, written as $\partial_x f(x,y)$ or $f_x(x,y)$ is defined as $$ f_x(x,y) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h,y) - f(x,y)}{h} $$ Similarly, the partial derivative of f with respect to y, written as $\partial_y f(x,y)$ or $f_y(x,y)$ is defined as $$ f_y(x,y) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x,y+h) - f(x,y)}{h} $$
Definition
Geometric Interpretation of Partial Derivatives
If $y=b$ is held fixed, the curve $C_1$ given by $z=f(x,b)$ is the trace of the surface $z=f(x,y)$ in the plane $y=b$. The partial derivative $f_x(a,b)$ is the slope of the line tangent to $C_1$ at the point $(a,b,f(a,b))$ — the rate of change of $z$ in the $x$-direction, with $y$ held fixed.

Likewise, if $x=a$ is held fixed, the curve $C_2$ given by $z=f(a,y)$ is the trace of the surface in the plane $x=a$, and $f_y(a,b)$ is the slope of the line tangent to $C_2$ at $(a,b,f(a,b))$ — the rate of change of $z$ in the $y$-direction, with $x$ held fixed.

Other common notations for these partial derivatives: $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a,b), \quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\bigg|_{(a,b)}, \quad z_x(a,b)$$
Example
Computing Partial Derivatives
Let $f(x,y) = x^2\sin(xy)$. Find $f_x(x,y)$ and $f_y(x,y)$.

To find $f_x$, treat $y$ as a constant and differentiate with respect to $x$, using the product rule together with the ordinary (single-variable) chain rule on $\sin(xy)$: $$f_x(x,y) = 2x\sin(xy) + x^2\cdot y\cos(xy) = 2x\sin(xy) + x^2y\cos(xy)$$ To find $f_y$, treat $x$ as a constant and differentiate with respect to $y$: $$f_y(x,y) = x^2\cdot x\cos(xy) = x^3\cos(xy)$$
Definition
Partial Derivatives of Functions of Three or More Variables
The idea extends directly to functions of three or more variables: to find the partial derivative with respect to one variable, hold every other variable fixed and differentiate as usual.

For $w=f(x,y,z)$, $$f_x(x,y,z) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h,y,z) - f(x,y,z)}{h}$$ and similarly for $f_y(x,y,z)$ and $f_z(x,y,z)$.
Example
Partial Derivatives with Three Variables
Let $f(x,y,z) = x^2y + y^2z + xz^2$. Find $f_x$, $f_y$, and $f_z$.

$$f_x(x,y,z) = 2xy + z^2$$ $$f_y(x,y,z) = x^2 + 2yz$$ $$f_z(x,y,z) = y^2 + 2xz$$
Definition
Higher-Order Partial Derivatives
The second-order partial derivatives of $f(x,y)$ are defined as $$f_{xx} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}$$ $$f_{yy} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}$$ $$f_{xy} = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y \partial x}$$ $$f_{yx} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\right) = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y}$$
Definition
Clairaut's Theorem
If $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ are continuous on an open disk, then for any point $(x,y)$ in the disk, $$f_{xy}(x,y) = f_{yx}(x,y)$$
Example
Verifying Clairaut's Theorem
Let $f(x,y) = e^{xy} + x^3y^2$. Verify that $f_{xy} = f_{yx}$.

$$f_x(x,y) = ye^{xy} + 3x^2y^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad f_{xy}(x,y) = e^{xy} + xye^{xy} + 6x^2y$$ $$f_y(x,y) = xe^{xy} + 2x^3y \quad\Rightarrow\quad f_{yx}(x,y) = e^{xy} + xye^{xy} + 6x^2y$$ Since $f_x$ and $f_y$ (and hence $f_{xy}$, $f_{yx}$) are continuous everywhere, Clairaut's theorem guarantees $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$ — and indeed the two computations agree.
Definition
From One Variable to Several: Motivating the Chain Rule
Recall the single-variable chain rule: if $y=f(x)$ and $x=g(t)$, then $\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dy}{dx}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt}$.

Now suppose $z=f(x,y)$, but $x$ and $y$ are themselves both functions of a single parameter $t$ — for instance, $(x(t),y(t))$ traces a path, and $z$ is the value of $f$ along that path. How should $\frac{dz}{dt}$ be computed?

What happens to $z$ as $t$ changes now comes from two sources at once: the change in $x$ and the change in $y$. This suggests the contributions from each need to be added together — which is exactly what the multivariable chain rule does.
Theorem
Chain Rule for One Independent Variable
Suppose $x=g(t)$ and $y=h(t)$ are differentiable functions of $t$, and $z=f(x,y)$ is a differentiable function of $x$ and $y$. Then $z=f(g(t),h(t))$ is a differentiable function of $t$, and $$\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\cdot\frac{dy}{dt}$$ where the ordinary derivatives are evaluated at $t$ and the partial derivatives are evaluated at $(x(t),y(t))$.
Example
Chain Rule for One Independent Variable
Let $z=x^2y+y^3$, where $x=\sin t$ and $y=e^t$. Find $\frac{dz}{dt}$.

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = 2xy, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = x^2+3y^2$$ $$\frac{dx}{dt} = \cos t, \qquad \frac{dy}{dt} = e^t$$ $$\frac{dz}{dt} = 2xy\cos t + (x^2+3y^2)e^t = 2e^t\sin t\cos t + (\sin^2 t + 3e^{2t})e^t$$ At $t=0$ (so $x=0$, $y=1$): $\dfrac{dz}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} = 0 + (0+3)(1) = 3$.
Theorem
Chain Rule for Two Independent Variables
Suppose $x=g(u,v)$ and $y=h(u,v)$ are differentiable functions of $u$ and $v$, and $z=f(x,y)$ is a differentiable function of $x$ and $y$. Then $z=f(g(u,v),h(u,v))$ is a differentiable function of $u$ and $v$, and $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial u} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial v} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial v}$$
Definition
Tree Diagrams
A tree diagram is a bookkeeping device for the chain rule: draw $z$ at the top, branching down to each intermediate variable it depends on ($x$, $y$), and each of those branching down to the independent variables they depend on ($u$, $v$).

  • $z \to x \to u$ contributes $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u}$
  • $z \to x \to v$ contributes $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v}$
  • $z \to y \to u$ contributes $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u}$
  • $z \to y \to v$ contributes $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v}$
To find $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial u}$, sum the contributions from every path in the tree that ends at $u$ — this is exactly the formula from the previous step. The same idea generalizes to any number of intermediate and independent variables: multiply the partial derivatives along each path from $z$ down to the target variable, then add the products over all such paths.
Example
Chain Rule for Two Independent Variables
Let $z=x^2+y^2$, where $x=uv$ and $y=u/v$. Find $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial u}$ and $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial v}$.

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=2x, \quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}=2y, \quad \frac{\partial x}{\partial u}=v,\quad \frac{\partial x}{\partial v}=u,\quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial u}=\frac1v,\quad \frac{\partial y}{\partial v}=-\frac{u}{v^2}$$ $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial u} = 2x\cdot v + 2y\cdot\frac1v = 2uv^2 + \frac{2u}{v^2}$$ $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial v} = 2x\cdot u + 2y\cdot\left(-\frac{u}{v^2}\right) = 2u^2v - \frac{2u^2}{v^3}$$
Definition
The Generalized Chain Rule
The same pattern holds with any number of intermediate variables $x_1,\dots,x_m$, each a differentiable function of independent variables $t_1,\dots,t_n$. If $w=f(x_1,\dots,x_m)$, then for each $t_i$, $$\frac{\partial w}{\partial t_i} = \sum_{k=1}^{m} \frac{\partial w}{\partial x_k}\cdot\frac{\partial x_k}{\partial t_i}$$ i.e. sum over every intermediate variable the tree diagram shows a path through, exactly as before — just with more branches.
Theorem
Implicit Differentiation of a Function of Two Variables
Suppose the equation $f(x,y)=0$ defines $y$ implicitly as a differentiable function $y=g(x)$ of $x$. Then $$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{\partial f/\partial x}{\partial f/\partial y} = -\frac{f_x(x,y)}{f_y(x,y)}$$ provided $f_y(x,y)\neq0$.

This follows from the one-variable chain rule applied to $f(x,y(x))=0$: differentiating both sides with respect to $x$ gives $f_x + f_y\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}=0$, and solving for $\frac{dy}{dx}$ gives the formula above.
Theorem
Implicit Differentiation of a Function of Three Variables
Likewise, if $f(x,y,z)=0$ defines $z$ implicitly as a differentiable function of $x$ and $y$, then $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\frac{f_x(x,y,z)}{f_z(x,y,z)}, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = -\frac{f_y(x,y,z)}{f_z(x,y,z)}$$ as long as $f_z(x,y,z)\neq0$.
Example
Implicit Differentiation
(a) The curve $x^3+y^3-6xy=0$ (the folium of Descartes) defines $y$ implicitly as a function of $x$. Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$.
(b) The sphere $x^2+y^2+z^2=1$ defines $z$ implicitly as a function of $x$ and $y$ near any point where $z\neq0$. Find $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}$ and $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}$.

(a) With $f(x,y)=x^3+y^3-6xy$: $f_x=3x^2-6y$, $f_y=3y^2-6x$, so $$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{3x^2-6y}{3y^2-6x} = \frac{2y-x^2}{y^2-2x}$$ (b) With $f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2-1$: $f_x=2x$, $f_y=2y$, $f_z=2z$, so $$\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\frac{x}{z}, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = -\frac{y}{z}$$
