Double Integrals

Double Integrals over Rectangular Regions
How much volume is under a surface?

A single integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) gives the area under a curve by adding up thin rectangles. For a function of two variables, the same idea gives volume under a surface \(z=f(x,y)\) above a rectangle \(R=[a,b]\times[c,d]\) in the \(xy\)-plane, by adding up thin boxes.

The surface here is \(z=27-2x^2-y^2\) over \([0,3]\times[0,3]\). Start with one box, then press Refine (or drag the slider) and watch the stack of boxes hug the surface. Drag the picture to rotate it.

What happens to the Riemann sum, and to the error, as the boxes get thinner?

The sum settles toward one number, \(162\), and the error shrinks toward \(0\). That limiting number is the double integral, the volume under the surface.

Definition
The double integral as a limit of Riemann sums

Split \(R=[a,b]\times[c,d]\) into \(m\times n\) sub-rectangles, each of width \(\Delta x=\dfrac{b-a}{m}\) and height \(\Delta y=\dfrac{d-c}{n}\). Pick a sample point \((x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\) in each. The double integral of \(f\) over \(R\) is

$$\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA=\lim_{m,n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n} f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A,\qquad \Delta A=\Delta x\,\Delta y.$$

Match each piece of the formula to the picture.

\(\Delta A\)the area of one sub-rectangle, the footprint of one box
\(f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\)the height of that box, read from the surface at the sample point
\(f(x_{ij}^*,y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A\)the volume of one box (height times base area)
\(\sum_i\sum_j\)add the volumes of all \(m\cdot n\) boxes
\(\lim_{m,n\to\infty}\)make the boxes infinitely thin so the stack matches the solid exactly

If \(f\) goes below the \(xy\)-plane, those boxes count as negative volume. The double integral is a signed volume.

Example 5.1: Approximating with Different Sample Points
\(f(x,y)=3x^2-y\) over \(R=[0,2]\times[0,2]\), with \(m=n=2\)

Switch between the sample-point rules below. Each box now has side \(1\), so \(\Delta A=1\). Red boxes are negative (the surface dips below the plane).

Upper-right corners: \((1,1),(2,1),(1,2),(2,2)\)\(f=2,\;11,\;1,\;10\), so the sum is \(24\)
Midpoints: \((0.5,0.5),(1.5,0.5),(0.5,1.5),(1.5,1.5)\)\(f=0.25,\;6.25,\;-0.75,\;5.25\), so the sum is \(11\)
The exact integral\(12\). Both estimates are off, because four boxes is far too coarse.
Which rule does better here?The midpoint rule: it misses by \(1\), and the corner rule by \(12\). Over-shoots on one side of a box tend to cancel with under-shoots on the other.

Now raise the sliders. Every rule heads for the same value, \(12\).

Theorem 5.1
Properties of double integrals

Assume \(f\) and \(g\) are integrable over the rectangle \(R\), \(S\) and \(T\) are subregions, and \(m,M\) are real numbers.

i. Sum\(\displaystyle\iint_R[f+g]\,dA=\iint_R f\,dA+\iint_R g\,dA\)
ii. Constant multiple\(\displaystyle\iint_R cf\,dA=c\iint_R f\,dA\)
iii. Splitting the regionIf \(R=S\cup T\) overlapping only on a boundary, \(\displaystyle\iint_R f\,dA=\iint_S f\,dA+\iint_T f\,dA\)
iv. ComparisonIf \(f\ge g\) on \(R\), then \(\displaystyle\iint_R f\,dA\ge\iint_R g\,dA\)
v. BoundsIf \(m\le f\le M\), then \(m\cdot A(R)\le\displaystyle\iint_R f\,dA\le M\cdot A(R)\)
vi. Product of separate functionsIf \(f(x,y)=g(x)h(y)\) on \(R=[a,b]\times[c,d]\), then \(\displaystyle\iint_R f\,dA=\left(\int_a^b g(x)\,dx\right)\left(\int_c^d h(y)\,dy\right)\)

Property v in words: the solid fits between two flat slabs. What are their volumes?

A slab of height \(m\) over \(R\) has volume \(m\cdot A(R)\), and a slab of height \(M\) has volume \(M\cdot A(R)\). The solid under the surface is trapped between them.

Fubini's Theorem
Iterated integrals, and slicing the solid

For \(R=[a,b]\times[c,d]\), an iterated integral integrates one variable at a time:

$$\int_a^b\!\!\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx=\int_a^b\left[\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\right]dx,\qquad \int_c^d\!\!\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=\int_c^d\left[\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\right]dy.$$

Fubini's Theorem. If \(f\) is continuous on \(R\), the double integral equals either iterated integral:

$$\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA=\int_a^b\!\!\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx=\int_c^d\!\!\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.$$

More generally the theorem holds when \(f\) is bounded on \(R\) and discontinuous only along a finite number of continuous curves.

Left: cut the solid with a plane at a fixed \(x\). The area of that cross-section is \(A(x)=\int_0^3 f(x,y)\,dy\). Right: \(A(x)\) plotted as the plane sweeps from \(x=0\) to \(x=2\); the shaded area is what has been collected so far. Press Sweep, then switch the order and sweep again.

Both orders end at the same total. What does that say about the volume of the solid?

It is one number, \(15\), however we slice it: by planes of constant \(x\) or by planes of constant \(y\). That is Fubini's theorem.

Examples 5.3 and 5.5
Splitting a sum, and a product of separate functions

Example 5.3. \(\displaystyle\iint_R (xy-3xy^2)\,dA\) over \(R=\{0\le x\le2,\;1\le y\le2\}\)

Split with property i and ii\(\displaystyle\iint_R xy\,dA-3\iint_R xy^2\,dA\)
First piece\(\displaystyle\int_1^2\!\!\int_0^2 xy\,dx\,dy=\int_1^2 2y\,dy=\left.y^2\right|_1^2=3\)
Second piece\(\displaystyle3\int_1^2\!\!\int_0^2 xy^2\,dx\,dy=3\int_1^2 2y^2\,dy=\left.2y^3\right|_1^2=14\)
Answer\(3-14=\mathbf{-11}\)

Negative is fine: over this region the \(-3xy^2\) term outweighs \(xy\).

Example 5.5. \(\displaystyle\iint_R e^{y}\cos x\,dA\) over \(R=\left[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right]\times[0,1]\)

The integrand is a function of \(x\) times a function of \(y\), so the double integral is a product of two single integrals.

The \(y\) factor\(\displaystyle\int_0^1 e^y\,dy=e-1\)
The \(x\) factor\(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos x\,dx=1\)
Answer\((e-1)(1)=\mathbf{e-1}\)
Example 5.7
Choosing the order wisely: \(\displaystyle\iint_R x\sin(xy)\,dA\) over \(R=[0,\pi]\times[1,2]\)

Both orders are legal, but one is much easier. Which variable should go first?

Integrate in \(y\) first\(\displaystyle\int_1^2 x\sin(xy)\,dy=\Big[-\cos(xy)\Big]_{y=1}^{y=2}=-\cos(2x)+\cos x\). The factor \(x\) cancels the chain-rule factor from the substitution \(u=xy\).
Then integrate in \(x\)\(\displaystyle\int_0^\pi\left(-\cos2x+\cos x\right)dx=\left[-\frac{\sin2x}{2}+\sin x\right]_0^\pi=0\)
What if we had integrated in \(x\) first?We would need integration by parts on \(x\sin(xy)\), then more work in \(y\). Same answer, much more effort.
Answer\(\mathbf{0}\)
Area, Volume, and Average Value
Using the double integral

Taking \(f(x,y)=1\) in the definition makes every box have height \(1\), so the "volume" equals the base area:

$$A(R)=\iint_R 1\,dA.$$

Example 5.8. Find the area of \(R=[0,3]\times[0,2]\).

\(\displaystyle\int_0^2\!\!\int_0^3 1\,dx\,dy=\int_0^2 3\,dy=\mathbf{6}\), as expected for a \(3\times2\) rectangle.

Example 5.9. Find the volume of the solid bounded above by the elliptic paraboloid \(2x^2+y^2+z=27\), below by the \(xy\)-plane, and on the sides by \(x=3\), \(y=3\), and the coordinate planes.

The height\(z=27-2x^2-y^2\) over \(R=[0,3]\times[0,3]\)
Inner integral (in \(x\))\(\displaystyle\int_0^3(27-2x^2-y^2)\,dx=\left[27x-\tfrac23x^3-xy^2\right]_0^3=63-3y^2\)
Outer integral (in \(y\))\(\displaystyle\int_0^3(63-3y^2)\,dy=\left.63y-y^3\right|_0^3=189-27=\mathbf{162}\)

Average value. \(f_{\text{ave}}=\dfrac{1}{A(R)}\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA\): the height of the flat box with the same volume.
