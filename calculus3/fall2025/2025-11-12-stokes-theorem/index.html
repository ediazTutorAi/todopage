Stokes' Theorem

Can Green’s theorem leave the plane?
Introduction to Stokes’ theorem

Stokes’ theorem is a higher-dimensional generalization of Green’s theorem. It relates a vector surface integral over a surface \(S\) in space to a line integral around the boundary of \(S\). Just like the theorems before it, it reduces an integral over a geometric object to an integral over its boundary.

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus\(\displaystyle\int_a^bf'(x)\,dx=\) \(f(b)-f(a)\)
Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals\(\displaystyle\int_C\nabla f\cdot d\vec r=\) \(f(\text{end})-f(\text{start})\)
Green’s theorem (circulation form)\(\displaystyle\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\) \(\displaystyle\iint_D\Big(\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial y}\Big)\,dA\)
Stokes’ theorem\(\displaystyle\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\) \(\displaystyle\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot d\vec S\)

Green’s theorem needs a flat region \(D\) in the \(xy\)-plane. What if the region is a curved surface, such as this dome? Its boundary \(C\) is the circle at the bottom.

Which integral could replace \(\iint_D\) over a flat region?A surface integral over \(S\): the flux of \(\operatorname{curl}\vec F\) through \(S\).
Which way around the boundary?
The positive orientation of \(C\)

Let \(S\) be an oriented smooth surface with unit normal vector \(\vec N\). Suppose the boundary of \(S\) is a simple closed curve \(C\). The orientation of \(S\) induces the positive orientation of \(C\) if, as you walk in the positive direction around \(C\) with your head pointing in the direction of \(\vec N\), the surface is always on your left.

Upward \(\vec N\): \(C\) runs counterclockwise seen from above. Press Flip.

Outward \(\vec N\) on the dome.

Curl the fingers of your right hand along \(C\). Where does your thumb point?Along \(\vec N\). This is the right-hand rule.
\(S\) is the unit disk in the \(xy\)-plane with \(\vec N=-\mathbf k\). Which way is \(C\) oriented?Clockwise seen from above (thumb pointing down).
Can Stokes’ theorem be used on a Möbius strip?No: it has no continuous unit normal, so it cannot be oriented, and then \(C\) has no positive orientation.
What does Stokes’ theorem say?
Theorem 6.19, Green’s theorem as a special case
Theorem 6.19: Stokes’ theorem

Let \(S\) be a piecewise smooth oriented surface with a boundary that is a simple closed curve \(C\) with positive orientation. If \(\vec F\) is a vector field with component functions that have continuous partial derivatives on an open region containing \(S\), then $$\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot d\vec S.$$

What does the left side measure?The circulation of \(\vec F\) around \(C\).
What does the right side measure?The flux of \(\operatorname{curl}\vec F\) through \(S\). Only the values of \(\vec F\) on the boundary matter.
Suppose \(S\) is a flat region in the \(xy\)-plane with upward orientation. Then \(\vec N=\mathbf k\) and \(d\vec S=\mathbf k\,dA\). What does the theorem say?\(\displaystyle\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot\mathbf k\,dA\): the circulation form of Green’s theorem. Green’s theorem is a special case of Stokes’ theorem.
If \(\vec F\) is conservative, what do both sides equal?\(0\): \(\operatorname{curl}\vec F=\vec 0\), and \(C\) is a closed curve.

Flat \(S\) in the \(xy\)-plane: Green’s theorem.

Curved \(S\) in space: Stokes’ theorem.

Why is it true?
The informal proof: chop \(S\) into small patches

Chop \(S\) into small pieces, each approximated by a square \(E\) with the orientation inherited from \(S\). Press the button to follow the argument.

Around one small square \(E\), Green’s theorem gives the circulation around its boundary. What is it, approximately?\(\displaystyle\oint_{\partial E}\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\iint_E\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot\vec N\,dS\), the flux of the curl through that patch.
Two neighboring squares share an edge. How is that edge traversed by each of them?In opposite directions: \(E_l=-F_r\). So the two line integrals along it cancel.
Add the circulations of all the small squares. What survives?Only the sides that approximate the boundary of \(S\): the sum is approximately \(\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r\). The sum of the fluxes is approximately \(\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot d\vec S\).
What happens as the squares shrink to zero?The approximations become exact: \(\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot d\vec S\).
Does it work? Verifying Stokes’ theorem
Example 6.73 and Checkpoint 6.61

Example 6.73: verify Stokes’ theorem for \(\vec F(x,y,z)=\langle y,\,2z,\,x^2\rangle\) and \(S\) the paraboloid \(z=4-x^2-y^2\), \(z\ge0\), outward oriented. Compute both sides, then press the button. Green arrows are \(\vec N\), orange arrows are \(\vec F\), the black curve is \(C\).

Surface side. \(\operatorname{curl}\vec F\)\(\operatorname{curl}\vec F=\langle R_y-Q_z,\ P_z-R_x,\ Q_x-P_y\rangle=\langle-2,\,-2x,\,-1\rangle\)
The normal: \(\vec r(x,y)=\langle x,y,4-x^2-y^2\rangle\), so \(\vec t_x\times\vec t_y\)\(\langle-g_x,-g_y,1\rangle=\langle2x,2y,1\rangle\) (upward, which is the outward orientation here)
The surface integral over \(D:\ x^2+y^2\le4\)\(\displaystyle\iint_D\langle-2,-2x,-1\rangle\cdot\langle2x,2y,1\rangle\,dA=\iint_D(-4x-4xy-1)\,dA=-\text{area}(D)=-4\pi\) (the odd terms integrate to \(0\))
Line side. Parameterize \(C\) with the positive orientation (counterclockwise seen from above).\(\vec r(t)=\langle2\cos t,\,2\sin t,\,0\rangle\), \(0\le t\le2\pi\)
\(\vec F(\vec r(t))\cdot\vec r'(t)\)\(\langle2\sin t,\,0,\,4\cos^2t\rangle\cdot\langle-2\sin t,\,2\cos t,\,0\rangle=-4\sin^2t\)
The line integral\(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}-4\sin^2t\,dt=-4\pi\). Both sides equal \(-4\pi\): Stokes’ theorem is verified.
Checkpoint 6.61. Verify Stokes’ theorem for \(\vec F=\langle y,x,-z\rangle\) and \(S\) the upwardly oriented portion of the graph of \(f(x,y)=x^2y\) over the triangle with vertices \((0,0)\), \((2,0)\), \((0,2)\).\(\operatorname{curl}\vec F=\langle0,0,1-1\rangle=\vec 0\), so the surface side is \(0\). \(\vec F=\nabla(xy-z^2/2)\) is conservative, so the line integral around the closed curve \(C\) is \(0\) too.
Surface integral to line integral
Example 6.74, surface independence and Checkpoint 6.62

Example 6.74: calculate \(\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot d\vec S\), where \(S\) is the surface on the left (a tube closed by a rounded end, open at the top) oriented outward, and \(\vec F=\langle z,\,2xy,\,x+y\rangle\).

\(S\): too complicated to parameterize.

\(S'\): the flat disk with the same boundary \(C\).

Can we use \(\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot(\vec t_u\times\vec t_v)\,dA\) directly?It needs a parameterization of \(S\), which is extremely difficult here. The boundary \(C\), however, is just a circle of radius 1.
By Stokes’ theorem, the integral equals \(\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r\). Parameterize \(C\) and compute \(\vec F\cdot\vec r'\).\(C:\ \vec r(t)=\langle\cos t,\sin t,1\rangle\). \(\vec F(\vec r(t))=\langle1,\,2\sin t\cos t,\,\cos t+\sin t\rangle\), so \(\vec F\cdot\vec r'=-\sin t+2\sin t\cos^2t\).
The value\(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}(-\sin t+2\sin t\cos^2t)\,dt=0\). (Reversing the orientation of \(C\) only changes the sign of \(0\).)
The flat disk \(S'\) has the same boundary and the same orientation. What is \(\iint_{S'}\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot d\vec S\)?Also \(0\): by Stokes’ theorem it equals the same line integral \(\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r\).
Surface independence (Equation 6.23)

Let \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) be smooth surfaces with the same boundary \(C\) and the same orientation. Then $$\iint_{S_1}\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot d\vec S=\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\iint_{S_2}\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot d\vec S.$$ Flux integrals of curl fields are surface independent in the same way that line integrals of gradient fields are path independent.

If \(\vec G=\operatorname{curl}\vec F\), on what does \(\iint_S\vec G\cdot d\vec S\) depend?Only on the boundary of \(S\), not on the surface itself.
\(S\) is a closed surface (an ellipsoid, say) and \(\vec F\) has continuous partial derivatives. What is \(\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot d\vec S\)?\(0\): a closed surface has no boundary curve. (Cut it along a curve into two pieces with opposite induced orientations on \(C\): the two line integrals cancel.)
Checkpoint 6.62. \(\vec F=\langle z,x,y\rangle\). \(S\) is a vase lying on its side, whose open circular end is the unit circle in the \(xz\)-plane centered at the origin. \(C\) is oriented clockwise when looking along the positive \(y\)-axis. Find \(\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot d\vec S\).\(\operatorname{curl}\vec F=\langle1,1,1\rangle\). Replace \(S\) by the flat disk \(x^2+z^2\le1\), \(y=0\). Clockwise looking along \(+y\) means \(\vec N=\mathbf j\). So the integral is \(\langle1,1,1\rangle\cdot\mathbf j\cdot\pi=\pi\).
Line integral to surface integral
Example 6.75 and Checkpoint 6.63

Example 6.75: calculate \(\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r\), where \(\vec F=\langle xy,\ x^2+y^2+z^2,\ yz\rangle\) and \(C\) is the boundary of the parallelogram with vertices \((0,0,1)\), \((0,1,0)\), \((2,1,-2)\), \((2,0,-1)\), taken counterclockwise seen from above (upward \(\vec N\)).

Directly, how many line integrals would we compute?Four, one for each side, each with its own parameterization. Not complicated, but time-consuming.
Use Stokes’ theorem instead. Which surface \(S\) has \(C\) as its boundary?The parallelogram itself: the portion of \(z=1-x-y\) over \(0\le x\le2\), \(0\le y\le1\). Parameterization \(\langle x,y,1-x-y\rangle\).
\(\operatorname{curl}\vec F\)\(\langle R_y-Q_z,\ P_z-R_x,\ Q_x-P_y\rangle=\langle-z,\,0,\,x\rangle\)
\(\vec t_x\times\vec t_y\), and the integrand \(\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot(\vec t_x\times\vec t_y)\)\(\langle1,1,1\rangle\), and \(\langle-(1-x-y),0,x\rangle\cdot\langle1,1,1\rangle=2x+y-1\).
The line integral\(\displaystyle\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\int_0^2\!\!\int_0^1(2x+y-1)\,dy\,dx=3\)
Checkpoint 6.63. \(\vec F=\langle z,x,y\rangle\), \(C\) oriented clockwise, the boundary of the triangle with vertices \((0,0,1)\), \((3,0,-2)\), \((0,1,2)\). Find \(\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r\).\(\operatorname{curl}\vec F=\langle1,1,1\rangle\). The triangle is \(z=1-x+y\) over the triangle \((0,0)\), \((3,0)\), \((0,1)\); upward \(\vec t_x\times\vec t_y=\langle1,-1,1\rangle\), so the integrand is \(1\) and the area is \(3/2\). Counterclockwise would give \(3/2\); clockwise gives \(-3/2\).
What does curl measure?
Stokes’ theorem and circulation per unit area
Let \(D_r\) be a small disk of radius \(r\) with center \(P_0\), and \(C_r\) its boundary circle. By Stokes’ theorem, \(\oint_{C_r}\vec F\cdot d\vec r=\) ?\(\displaystyle\iint_{D_r}\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot\vec N\,dS\approx\iint_{D_r}(\operatorname{curl}\vec F)(P_0)\cdot\vec N(P_0)\,dS\), because the curl is continuous.
The quantity \((\operatorname{curl}\vec F)(P_0)\cdot\vec N(P_0)\) is constant on \(D_r\). So \(\oint_{C_r}\vec F\cdot d\vec r\approx\) ?\(\pi r^2\,[(\operatorname{curl}\vec F)(P_0)\cdot\vec N(P_0)]\), and the approximation gets arbitrarily close as \(r\to0\).
Solve for the curl component and let \(r\to0^+\).\(\displaystyle(\operatorname{curl}\vec F)(P_0)\cdot\vec N(P_0)=\lim_{r\to0^+}\frac{1}{\pi r^2}\oint_{C_r}\vec F\cdot d\vec r\): the curl is the circulation per unit area.
If \(\vec F\) is the velocity field of a fluid, about which axis \(\vec N\) is the rotation largest?The axis along \(\operatorname{curl}\vec F\): a tiny paddlewheel at \(P_0\) turns fastest when its axis points in the direction of \(\operatorname{curl}\vec F\). Stokes’ theorem justifies this interpretation of the curl.
Optional: Faraday’s law
Example 6.76 and Checkpoint 6.64

Let \(C(t)\) be a closed curve that models a thin wire, \(D(t)\) a surface with boundary \(C(t)\) (positive orientation), and \(\vec B(t)\) a magnetic field. The flux \(\phi(t)=\iint_{D(t)}\vec B(t)\cdot d\vec S\) creates an electric field \(\vec E(t)\) that does work.

Faraday’s law

Integral form: \(\displaystyle\text{Work}=\oint_{C(t)}\vec E(t)\cdot d\vec r=-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}\).

Differential form: \(\displaystyle\operatorname{curl}\vec E=-\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}\).

Apply Stokes’ theorem to the integral form.\(\displaystyle-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}=\oint_{C(t)}\vec E\cdot d\vec r=\iint_{D(t)}\operatorname{curl}\vec E\cdot d\vec S\)
Since \(\phi(t)=\iint_{D(t)}\vec B(t)\cdot d\vec S\), and the surface does not vary with time, what is \(-\partial\phi/\partial t\)?\(\displaystyle\iint_{D(t)}-\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}\cdot d\vec S\). So \(\displaystyle\iint_{D(t)}-\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}\cdot d\vec S=\iint_{D(t)}\operatorname{curl}\vec E\cdot d\vec S\).
Do equal integrals force equal integrands? Compare \(\int_0^1x\,dx\) and \(\int_0^1f\,dx\) with \(f=1\) on \([0,\tfrac12]\), \(f=0\) on \((\tfrac12,1]\).Both equal \(\tfrac12\), but \(x\ne f(x)\). Here, however, the equality holds for every surface, however small. Shrinking \(D(t)\) to a point gives \(\operatorname{curl}\vec E=-\partial\vec B/\partial t\).
Example 6.76. The magnetic field is constant, \(\vec B(t)=\langle1,-4,2\rangle\). Find \(\operatorname{curl}\vec E\).\(\partial\vec B/\partial t=\vec 0\), so \(\operatorname{curl}\vec E=\vec 0\): the curl of the electric field of a constant magnetic field is always zero.
Checkpoint 6.64. \(\vec B(t)=\langle tx,\,ty,\,-2tz\rangle\). Find \(\operatorname{curl}\vec E\).\(\operatorname{curl}\vec E=-\partial\vec B/\partial t=\langle-x,\,-y,\,2z\rangle\). It does not change over time, although \(\vec B\) does.
Summary and practice
Exercises 335, 343, 344, 347 and two common mistakes

Stokes’ theorem: the circulation of \(\vec F\) around the edge of \(S\) equals the total curl of \(\vec F\) passing through \(S\).

Want \(\displaystyle\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot d\vec S\)Parameterize \(S\) directly, or compute \(\displaystyle\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r\) on the boundary, or switch to an easier surface with the same boundary and orientation.
Want \(\displaystyle\oint_C\vec F\cdot d\vec r\) for a closed curve \(C\)Pick a surface \(S\) with boundary \(C\) (a flat one if possible) and compute \(\displaystyle\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot d\vec S\).
Checklist(1) Is \(C\) closed and the boundary of \(S\)? (2) Orientation: right-hand rule. (3) \(\operatorname{curl}\vec F\). (4) \(\vec t_u\times\vec t_v\) (or \(\langle-s_x,-s_y,1\rangle\) for a graph) and the integrand. (5) Check the sign against the orientation.
335. \(\displaystyle\oint_C\big[2xy^2z\,dx+2x^2yz\,dy+(x^2y^2-2z)\,dz\big]\), \(C:\ x=\cos t,\ y=\sin t,\ z=\sin t\), \(0\le t\le2\pi\).\(\vec F=\nabla(x^2y^2z-z^2)\) is conservative, so \(\operatorname{curl}\vec F=\vec 0\) and the integral is \(0\).
343. \(\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot\vec N\,dS\), \(\vec F=\langle z^2,y^2,x\rangle\), \(S\) the triangle with vertices \((1,0,0)\), \((0,1,0)\), \((0,0,1)\), upward.\(\operatorname{curl}\vec F=\langle0,2z-1,0\rangle\). With \(z=1-x-y\), \(\vec t_x\times\vec t_y=\langle1,1,1\rangle\): \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{1-x}(1-2x-2y)\,dy\,dx=-\dfrac16\).
344. \(\displaystyle\oint_C(z\,dx+x\,dy+y\,dz)\), \(C\) the triangle with vertices \((3,0,0)\), \((0,0,2)\), \((0,6,0)\) traversed in that order.\(\operatorname{curl}\vec F=\langle1,1,1\rangle\). The order of the vertices gives the normal \(\langle-12,-6,-18\rangle\) (pointing away from the first octant). The flux is \(\tfrac12\langle1,1,1\rangle\cdot\langle-12,-6,-18\rangle=-18\).
347. \(\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot\vec N\,dS\), \(\vec F=\langle z,3x,2z\rangle\), \(S:\ z=1-x^2-y^2\), \(z\ge0\), upward.Use the boundary: \(C\) is \(x^2+y^2=1\), \(z=0\), counterclockwise. \(\vec F\cdot d\vec r=3x\,dy=3\cos^2t\,dt\), so \(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}3\cos^2t\,dt=3\pi\).
Spot the mistake. A student computes \(\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot d\vec S\) for the upward-oriented dome \(S\) by taking the boundary circle \(C\) clockwise seen from above.With upward \(\vec N\), the positive direction of \(C\) is counterclockwise seen from above. Clockwise gives the opposite sign.
Spot the mistake. A student computes \(\iint_S\operatorname{curl}\vec F\cdot d\vec S\) over the whole unit sphere by using the equator as \(C\).The sphere is closed: it has no boundary. The equator is the boundary of a hemisphere. Over the whole (closed) sphere the integral is \(0\).
