Triple Integrals

Adding a Third Variable
From area under a surface to mass of a solid

A double integral adds up a value \(f\) times a small area. If \(f(x,y,z)\) is a quantity spread through a solid (a density, a temperature), each piece we add up needs a small volume instead.

The box \(B=[0,2]\times[0,2]\times[0,2]\) with \(f(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2\). Press Refine: it is cut into \(l\times m\times n\) small boxes, and each contributes \(f\) at its center times its volume \(\Delta V\). Drag the picture to turn it.

If \(f\) is a density (g/cm\(^3\)), what does \(\sum f\,\Delta V\) estimate?The mass of the solid: density times volume for each little box, added up.
What does the sum give if \(f=1\)?\(\sum \Delta V\), the volume of the solid. So a triple integral of \(1\) is a volume.
What number are the sums settling to?\(32\). That is the exact value of \(\iiint_B (x^2+y^2+z^2)\,dV\); the sum with one big box is far off, and the error shrinks as the boxes do.

Goal for today: compute that limit without cutting anything, by integrating one variable at a time.

Definition and Fubini's Theorem
Theorem 5.9: a box can be sliced in any order

Split \(B=[a,b]\times[c,d]\times[e,f]\) into \(l\,m\,n\) subboxes with \(\Delta x=\tfrac{b-a}{l},\ \Delta y=\tfrac{d-c}{m},\ \Delta z=\tfrac{f-e}{n}\), so each has volume \(\Delta V=\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z\). Choosing a sample point \((x^*_{ijk},y^*_{ijk},z^*_{ijk})\) in each,

$$\iiint_B f(x,y,z)\,dV=\lim_{l,m,n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{l}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x^*_{ijk},y^*_{ijk},z^*_{ijk})\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z .$$

If \(f\) is continuous on \(B\) the limit exists. Fubini (Theorem 5.9): then \(\iiint_B f\,dV\) equals an iterated integral in any of the \(3!=6\) orders, worked from the inside out. On a box every order has constant limits. Which iterated integral goes with each order (innermost variable first)?

\(dx,\ dy,\ dz\)\(\displaystyle\int_e^f\!\!\int_c^d\!\!\int_a^b f\,dx\,dy\,dz\)
\(dx,\ dz,\ dy\)\(\displaystyle\int_c^d\!\!\int_e^f\!\!\int_a^b f\,dx\,dz\,dy\)
\(dy,\ dz,\ dx\)\(\displaystyle\int_a^b\!\!\int_e^f\!\!\int_c^d f\,dy\,dz\,dx\)
\(dy,\ dx,\ dz\)\(\displaystyle\int_e^f\!\!\int_a^b\!\!\int_c^d f\,dy\,dx\,dz\)
\(dz,\ dx,\ dy\)\(\displaystyle\int_c^d\!\!\int_a^b\!\!\int_e^f f\,dz\,dx\,dy\)
\(dz,\ dy,\ dx\)\(\displaystyle\int_a^b\!\!\int_c^d\!\!\int_e^f f\,dz\,dy\,dx\)

Same rule as before: the limits of the outermost integral are always constants, and the variable you integrate last is the one you sliced along.

Example 5.36: A Box, Inside Out
Slices: why the middle answer is a function of z

Example 5.36. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iiint_B (x+yz^2)\,dV\) over \(B=\{-1\le x\le5,\ 2\le y\le4,\ 0\le z\le1\}\), integrating in the order \(dx,\ dy,\ dz\).

Sweep the plane \(z=t\) through the box. The slice is a rectangle in the \(xy\)-plane, and the readout \(A(z)=\iint f\,dx\,dy\) is exactly what the two inner integrals produce.

Inner integral, in \(x\)\(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{5}(x+yz^2)\,dx=\Big[\tfrac{x^2}{2}+xyz^2\Big]_{-1}^{5}=12+6yz^2\)
Next, in \(y\)\(\displaystyle\int_2^4(12+6yz^2)\,dy=\big[12y+3y^2z^2\big]_2^4=24+36z^2\)
Last, in \(z\)\(\displaystyle\int_0^1(24+36z^2)\,dz=24+12=\mathbf{36}\)
Compare with the readout\(A(z)=24+36z^2\) is the slice integral: at \(z=0\) it reads \(24\), at \(z=1\) it reads \(60\). The final integral adds up the slices.
General Solids: A Pillar over the Base
Theorem 5.10

For a solid \(E\) that is not a box, suppose \(D\) is its shadow on the \(xy\)-plane and \(E\) lies between two surfaces: \(E=\{(x,y,z)\mid (x,y)\in D,\ u_1(x,y)\le z\le u_2(x,y)\}\). Then

$$\iiint_E f\,dV=\iint_D\Big[\int_{u_1(x,y)}^{u_2(x,y)}f(x,y,z)\,dz\Big]dA .$$

Example: the tetrahedron bounded by \(x=0,\ y=0,\ z=0\) and \(x+y+z=1\), with \(f=5x-3y\). The red pillar stands over one point \((x,y)\) of \(D\) (yellow) and runs from the floor to the slanted face. Move its \(x\) and \(y\) and read off where \(z\) starts and stops.

What are \(u_1\) and \(u_2\)?\(u_1=0\) (the floor) and \(u_2=1-x-y\) (the plane \(x+y+z=1\) solved for \(z\)).
What is \(D\)?Set \(z=0\) in the slanted face: the triangle \(x+y\le1\) in the first quadrant, so \(0\le x\le1,\ 0\le y\le1-x\).
What does the inner integral compute?One pillar's worth: \(\int_0^{1-x-y}f\,dz\) for a fixed \((x,y)\). The outer double integral then adds up every pillar over \(D\).

Project onto the \(xz\)- or \(yz\)-plane instead and the same theorem holds with the roles of the variables swapped.

Example 5.38: The Tetrahedron
Three nested limits from one picture

Example 5.38. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iiint_E(5x-3y)\,dV\) for the tetrahedron of the last step. Write \(E=\{0\le x\le1,\ 0\le y\le1-x,\ 0\le z\le1-x-y\}\). Sweep the plane \(x=t\): the slice is a triangle that shrinks to a point.

Set up\(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{1-x}\!\!\int_0^{1-x-y}(5x-3y)\,dz\,dy\,dx\)
Inner, in \(z\)\((5x-3y)(1-x-y)\), since the integrand does not contain \(z\).
Middle, in \(y\)\(\displaystyle\int_0^{1-x}(5x-3y)(1-x-y)\,dy=\tfrac12(x-1)^2(6x-1)\)
Outer, in \(x\)\(\displaystyle\int_0^1\tfrac12(x-1)^2(6x-1)\,dx=\tfrac{1}{12}\)
Where do you see the middle answer?It is \(A(x)\) in the readout. At \(x=0\) it reads \(-0.5\): the slice is the biggest triangle, and \(f=-3y\) is negative there, so it contributes negative volume.
Volume as a Triple Integral
Example 5.39: a square pyramid

With \(f=1\) the triple integral is the volume: \(V(E)=\iiint_E 1\,dV\).

Example 5.39. The pyramid with base \([-1,1]\times[-1,1]\) in the \(xy\)-plane and apex \((0,0,1)\). Sweep the plane \(z=t\) upward.

What shape is each slice, and how big?A square. At height \(z\) its sides run from \(-1+z\) to \(1-z\), so the side is \(2(1-z)\) and \(A(z)=4(1-z)^2\).
Write \(E\) so that \(z\) is outermost\(E=\{0\le z\le1,\ -1+z\le y\le1-z,\ -1+z\le x\le1-z\}\)
Volume\(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_{-1+z}^{1-z}\!\!\int_{-1+z}^{1-z}dx\,dy\,dz=\int_0^1 4(1-z)^2dz=\tfrac{\mathbf4}{\mathbf3}\)
Sanity check\(\tfrac13\cdot(\text{base area }4)\cdot(\text{height }1)=\tfrac43\). The familiar pyramid formula.

Slicing by \(z\) was the only reasonable choice: the top surface \(z=1-\max(|x|,|y|)\) has a crease, but every horizontal slice is a plain square.

Example 5.40: Changing the Order
Same solid, three different shadows

Example 5.40. The integral \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{x^2}\!\!\int_0^{y^2}xyz\,dz\,dy\,dx\) is over \(E=\{0\le x\le1,\ 0\le y\le x^2,\ 0\le z\le y^2\}\). Switch the slicing axis: each choice is a different order of integration for the same solid, and the total never changes.

Evaluate as given\(\int_0^{y^2}xyz\,dz=\tfrac12xy^5\), then \(\int_0^{x^2}\tfrac12xy^5dy=\tfrac{x^{13}}{12}\), then \(\int_0^1\tfrac{x^{13}}{12}dx=\tfrac1{168}\).
Now integrate \(dx\) first. Find the \(x\)-limits at a fixed \((y,z)\).From \(y\le x^2\) we get \(x\ge\sqrt y\), and \(x\le1\). So \(\sqrt y\le x\le1\).
The \(yz\)-shadow\(0\le y\le1,\ 0\le z\le y^2\), giving \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{y^2}\!\!\int_{\sqrt y}^{1}xyz\,dx\,dz\,dy\)
Evaluate it\(\int_{\sqrt y}^1xyz\,dx=\tfrac12yz(1-y)\), then \(\int_0^{y^2}\to\tfrac14y^5(1-y)\), then \(\int_0^1\to\tfrac14\big(\tfrac16-\tfrac17\big)=\tfrac1{168}\). Same answer.
Common mistakeWriting \(y\le x\le1\) instead of \(\sqrt y\le x\le1\). Solve the inequality \(y\le x^2\) for \(x\); do not just rename the bounds. That slip gives \(\tfrac1{96}\).
Example 5.41: A Paraboloid and Polar Coordinates
When the shadow is a disk

Example 5.41. Let \(E\) be the solid bounded by \(y=x^2+z^2\) and \(y=4\). Evaluate \(\displaystyle\iiint_E\sqrt{x^2+z^2}\,dV\). The paraboloid opens along the \(y\)-axis, so project onto the \(xz\)-plane. Sweep \(y\): each slice is a disk.

The shadow \(D\) in the \(xz\)-planeWhere the surfaces meet: \(x^2+z^2=4\). So \(D\) is the disk \(x^2+z^2\le4\), and \(x^2+z^2\le y\le4\).
Set up with \(dy\) innermost\(\displaystyle\iint_D\int_{x^2+z^2}^{4}\sqrt{x^2+z^2}\,dy\,dA=\iint_D(4-x^2-z^2)\sqrt{x^2+z^2}\,dA\)
Convert \(D\) to polar (\(x=r\cos\theta,\ z=r\sin\theta\))\(\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^2(4-r^2)\,r\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta\). The extra \(r\) is the polar \(dA\).
Evaluate\(\displaystyle\int_0^2(4r^2-r^4)\,dr=\tfrac{64}{15}\), times \(2\pi\): \(\ \mathbf{\tfrac{128\pi}{15}}\approx26.81\)
Slice check at \(y=4\)\(A(4)=\iint_{r\le2}r\,dA=2\pi\cdot\tfrac{2^3}{3}=\tfrac{16\pi}{3}\approx16.8\), the value the readout shows at the end of the sweep.
Average Value
Theorem 5.11 and Example 5.42

The average value of \(f\) over a solid \(E\) with volume \(V(E)>0\) is total divided by size:

$$f_{\text{ave}}=\frac{1}{V(E)}\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV .$$

Example 5.42. The temperature at \((x,y,z)\) is \(T=xy+8z+20\) (\(^\circ\)C) in the tetrahedron \(x+y+z\le1\), \(x,y,z\ge0\). Find the average temperature. The readout shows both pieces.

Volume of \(E\)\(V=\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac12\cdot1=\tfrac16\)
\(\displaystyle\iiint_E T\,dV\)Inner \(dz\): \((xy+20)(1-x-y)+4(1-x-y)^2\). Then \(dy\) and \(dx\) give \(\tfrac{147}{40}\).
Average temperature\(T_{\text{ave}}=6\cdot\tfrac{147}{40}=\tfrac{441}{20}=\mathbf{22.05^\circ C}\)
Is it plausible?Yes. \(T\) ranges from \(20\) (at the origin face) to \(28\) (at the top vertex), and most of the volume is near the base, so the average sits just above \(20\).
